Implementation guide

Health and labour supply module for IndoTERM

Construction, implementation and simulation procedure

This guide sets out how to introduce health status into the IndoTERM model of Indonesia and simulate the effect of energy transition policies on health, labour supply and output. The new work is three pieces: a labour database built from the household surveys, a re-aggregated economic database, and about forty equations added to the model. Two parts are difficult: calibrating the transition matrix (task B.8) and writing the module code (tasks D.3 to D.7). Everything else is a short preparation step, a machine run, or a check.

The procedure is written as six stages. Each task states what to do, the condition that shows it is complete, and its known failure modes. The tasks are numerous because the checks are listed separately; each check takes minutes and prevents an error that would cost days. Every stage ends with a completion gate.

How to use this guide
  • To execute: read section 4, then start at Stage A (section 6). Read section 5 before Stage B.
  • To understand the module first: read section 5 in full, including the three interactive figures.
  • To check a single task: every task names its inputs by task number, so it can be read on its own.

1Purpose and scope

The host model records how much of each fuel each industry, household group and region burns. It does not represent the effect of combustion on health, nor the effect of health on labour supply. The module supplies that representation.

The module classifies the working-age population by health status and labour-market status, and moves the population between these classes from one year to the next. A policy that changes the incidence of illness therefore changes labour supply, and through labour supply it changes output, wages and employment by region.

Within scope. The module database; the changes to the economic database; the module equations; the baseline and policy simulations and their analysis.

Outside scope. The relation between emissions in one province and ambient concentration in another, and the relation between concentration and disease incidence. These are inputs to the module. Task D.7 specifies their interface; their estimation is not treated here.

5Theoretical basis of the module

Stage B builds numerical arrays. They make sense only once the specification they serve is understood. This section states the specification and gives three interactive figures in which the governing parameter can be varied and the response observed. The values in the figures are illustrative, not estimates for Indonesia.

At the start of each year every person of working age occupies a category: region, age, sex, health status, and the activity of the preceding year. During the year each category makes offers: to stay in the same occupation, to move to another, to be unemployed, or to leave the labour force. Industry labour demand sets the number of positions. Persons who held a position keep it first; the remaining positions are vacancies and are shared among the other offers. At the close of the year the transition matrix advances the population one year in age, moves a proportion between health states, and removes those who die. The result is next year's categories.

Health enters at a single point: the transition matrix. Everything else follows from that entry.

5.1The governing equations

The published specification has forty-two equations (Appendix C lists them). Six carry the whole mechanism; the rest are accounting and percentage-change forms of these. Region and sex indices are suppressed here for legibility; every array also runs over them.

E.1CAT(o,a,h)=∑aa∑hhACT_L(o,aa,hh)⋅T(o,aa,hh,a,h)

Categories. This year's categories are last year's activities moved by the transition matrix. The activity label o is carried over; only age and health status change. T is the sole point at which health enters (task D.3).

E.6L(c,a)=CAT(c)⋅[(B(c,a)⋅ATW(a))σ∑q(B(c,q)⋅ATW(q))σ]

Offers. Each category c allocates its members among destinations a by a CES share. B is the preference weight, ATW the real after-tax wage, σ the elasticity of substitution, set to 2 (η in G-241). Poor health lowers B on every working destination (Figure 4, task D.4).

E.14, E.16VAC(o)=LD(o)−H(o,o)H(c,o)=VAC(o)⋅[L(c,o)OfferTo(o)], OfferTo(o)=∑c≠oL(c,o)

Vacancies and rationing. Vacancies are labour demand less incumbents, the people who keep the position they held. Vacancies are shared among the categories offering to o from outside o, in proportion to their offers (task D.5).

E.33ATWt(o)policyATWt(o)base−ATWt−1(o)policyATWt−1(o)base=α[LDt(o)policyLDt(o)base−LSt(o)policyLSt(o)base]

Sticky real wage. In a policy run the deviation of the real wage from baseline moves each year by α times the gap between the deviations of labour demand and labour supply; α is 0.5 to 0.6 (Figure 5, task D.6).

E.36 to E.42New(a)=∑o∑aaACT_L(o,aa,healthy)⋅T(o,aa,healthy,a,ill)

New cases. New cases are the healthy population times the onset rate. In the baseline a shift on that rate is made endogenous so that new cases follow an imposed path; in a policy run the shift is exogenous and carries the deviation from the pollution calculation (task D.7).

extensionLS(o)eff=∑he(h)⋅ACT(o,h)

Effective labour. Labour supplied to occupation o is the number employed, weighted by the effective labour coefficient of each health status. Not in the published specification (tasks B.9, D.8).

5.2Allocation of labour offers

Each category divides its members between destinations by a constant elasticity of substitution share function. The share going to a destination rises with the real after-tax wage there and with a preference weight. Poor health lowers the preference weight on every working destination at once, so the share leaving the labour force rises.

Interactive figure

Figure 4Allocation of offers under the CES share function

The starting distribution reproduces the published assumptions: 88 per cent stay, 7 per cent move occupation, 3 per cent enter unemployment. Adjust the controls and observe the response.

The point. The health control moves all three working destinations together and raises the share leaving the labour force. That gradient sets the size of the policy result, and the household survey measures it directly (task B.5).

5.3Wage adjustment

The real wage does not clear the labour market within the year. Each year it moves by a fraction α of the gap between labour demand and labour supply, both relative to baseline. Until the adjustment is complete, extra labour supply appears as unemployment, not as employment.

Interactive figure

Figure 5Response to a permanent one per cent rise in labour supply

Labour supply rises by one per cent in year one and stays there. The series show how far employment and the real wage are from baseline in each later year.

Employment Real wage

The point. α sets only how fast the economy gets to the end point, not where the end point is. The years of unemployment before the gain appears are the transition cost. Published applications use α of 0.5 to 0.6; both are tested in task E.8.

5.4Identification of transition rates without longitudinal data

The transition matrix needs two rates: the annual probability that a healthy person becomes ill, and the annual probability that an ill person recovers. No Indonesian survey follows the same person across years, so neither rate is observed. But the proportion of the population that is ill at each age is the accumulated result of both rates over a lifetime. Annual cross-sections of that proportion therefore identify both rates together.

Interactive figure

Figure 6Recovery of the two rates from the age profile

The points are a target profile generated from a known pair of rates. Adjust the two controls until the line passes through the points. Reaching them shows that both rates are identified from the age profile alone. This is task B.8 in miniature.

Simulated profile Target profile

The point. At young ages a high incidence with high recovery looks almost the same as a low incidence with low recovery. The curvature at older ages separates them. Use the full age range, then scale the level to an external total (task B.8).

TASK A.5 Check

ProcedureOpen the common closure file and the baseline shock file. For each statement, write one sentence stating what it fixes and what it frees. Four statements matter for the present work.

! capital accumulates through investment rather than being fixed

swap finv1 = finv4;
! THIS STATEMENT IS REPLACED IN TASK E.1.

VerificationThe closure and shock check passes without starting a simulation. The check reads the model, applies the closure, and confirms that exogenous variables equal equations and that every shocked variable is exogenous.

Definition of a closure

The model has more variables than equations. A closure is the set of variables fixed from outside. The same model with a different closure is a different experiment. A swap exchanges the status of two variables: the first becomes endogenous, the second exogenous. The swaps above are what turn a one-period model into a model solved year by year.

7Stage B. Construction of the labour database

This stage turns three data sources into the arrays of Table 2. The method is the one used for South Africa: assume the shape of each rate, then scale its level to an external total that can be trusted.

Input Susenas, Sakernas, GBD, BPS Output the module arrays in one model data file Duration months, one research assistant
Requirement for the whole stage

Every value that is not measured goes into a register with its value, its basis, and the external total it was scaled to. The register is a project deliverable and is what allows a reviewer to assess the module. Task B.10 compiles it; fill it in as the stage proceeds.

TASK B.1 Prepare

Define the module sets

ProcedureFix the five set definitions in Table 6 and record the reason for each. Changing a set later means rebuilding every array.

VerificationThe activity set matches the model's occupation set exactly. Inspect the occupation set in the database before any survey processing; the module must supply those occupations and no others.

Treatment of informal employment

About half of Indonesian employment is informal. An informal worker who falls ill usually works fewer hours rather than stopping work. If informal and formal workers are classified the same way, the health effect on half the workforce is invisible. This is the main departure from the published specification.

TASK B.2 Prepare

Specify the data request

ProcedureIssue three requests, each naming the blocks and the level of aggregation.

  • Susenas March core, 2018 to 2025, person level: the functional-difficulty, health-complaint and labour blocks, harmonised across years.
  • Susenas March core, 2013 to 2017: the last rounds that record days of activity lost to illness. Needed once, for task B.9.
  • Sakernas: occupation, earnings, start date of current job, and cessation of work in the last year with reason.

VerificationEach March file holds about 1.1 million person records. A file of a few hundred thousand is the September module, whose sample is about a quarter of the size.

The 2018 questionnaire change that shapes the design

Up to 2017 the form recorded days of activity lost to illness. From 2018 that item was dropped and a block of eight graded functional-difficulty items was added, asked of everyone aged two and above. So the health status variable exists from 2018 onward, and the hours-lost parameter comes from 2013 to 2017 and is carried forward.

TASK B.3 Build

Construct the activities table

ProcedureProduce one weighted table: population by region, age, sex, health status and activity. Two coding rules are needed and both must be written down. Health status: the most severe response across the eight domains, reduced to three states. Activity: employed with occupation and formal or informal status; unemployed, short or long term; outside the labour force; new entrant.

* Outline only. Variable names change between rounds;
* consult the codebook for the year being processed.

* 1. health status: most severe response across the eight domains
egen  worst = rowmax(diff1 diff2 diff3 diff4 diff5 diff6 diff7 diff8)
recode worst (1=1 "no difficulty") (2=2 "some difficulty") ///
             (3/4=3 "severe difficulty"), gen(h)

* 2. activity, from the labour block
gen     act = .
replace act = occ_group   if employed==1 & formal==1
replace act = occ_group+4 if employed==1 & formal==0
replace act = 9  if unemployed==1 & months_searching<=12
replace act = 10 if unemployed==1 & months_searching>12
replace act = 11 if new_entrant==1
replace act = 12 if not_in_labour_force==1

* 3. weighted table
collapse (sum) people=weight, by(region age sex h act)

VerificationThe table reproduces the published BPS totals. Summed over health and activity it gives the population by province; summed over health alone it gives published employment and unemployment. A mismatch means the weight variable or the activity coding is wrong.

CautionSome cells are nearly empty, for example young persons with severe difficulty in one occupation. List them now and pool them in task B.5, rather than let a handful of records set a rate.

Comparison with the South African application

South Africa had no joint observation of health and work status. National prevalence came from an external model and was spread across occupations by assumed shares. Susenas records both for the same person, with region and age. This one table replaces the least defensible step of the published procedure.

TASK B.4 Prepare

Derive lagged activities, categories and new entrants

ProcedureThree operations in order. (1) Lagged activities: divide the activities table by one plus labour-force growth. (2) Categories: premultiply lagged activities by the transition matrix; this waits for task B.8 and is completed on return. (3) New entrants: the number of persons aged 15 by province from the BPS projections, which is cleaner than a residual.

VerificationCategories plus new entrants equal activities when each is summed over all its classes. A mismatch means the transition matrix rows do not sum correctly (task B.8).

TASK B.5 Build

Construct the offer table and calibrate the health gradient

ProcedureThe offer table records what people intend before positions are rationed. Build it by the four rules in Table 7. The last rule is the one that determines the policy result: compute the employment rate by health status, age, sex and region from the table in task B.3, and set the offer strengths so that the module reproduces those rates. South Africa assumed this gradient; here it is measured.

VerificationEach row of the offer table sums to its category size. Column totals will not match observed employment, because offers are intentions; task B.6 establishes that match.

TASK B.6 Build

Derive realised flows by vacancy iteration

ProcedurePositions are limited, so most offers are refused. Vacancies and retained positions determine each other, so iterate to convergence: (1) assume three per cent of employees are dismissed, unless Sakernas shows otherwise; (2) count persons who keep last year's position; (3) vacancies are labour demand less those persons; (4) share vacancies among the other offers in proportion to the offers; (5) recount retention given the moves, and return to (2).

VerificationThe loop converges and the column totals reproduce the observed totals of employment, unemployment and non-participation from task B.3. Non-convergence means the offer strengths of task B.5 are inconsistent with the number of positions.

TASK B.7 Prepare

Construct the ageing and mortality part of the transition matrix

ProcedureThis part is looked up, not calibrated. With ten-year age groups, about ten per cent of each group moves up each year. Take mortality by age, sex and province from the BPS life tables and check it against the Global Burden of Disease.

VerificationApplying this part to the current population reproduces next year's BPS projection. A material gap means the module's age groups and the life table's age groups are not aligned.

TASK B.8 Build Difficult

Calibrate the health part of the transition matrix

The most demanding task in Stage B. Figure 6 is the same problem in miniature; work through it first.

ProcedureFour steps. (1) Shape of incidence: take incidence by cause, age and sex for Indonesia from the Global Burden of Disease and map the causes to the three difficulty states; respiratory and cardiovascular disease matter most because air pollution changes them. (2) Recovery is not measured anywhere; treat it as the parameter to find. (3) Calibrate: choose recovery and adjust incidence so that the proportion with a difficulty at each age matches Susenas in each year 2018 to 2025. (4) Scale the level: multiply both rates by one factor so that national cases by cause match the Global Burden of Disease total. Calibration fixes the shape; this step fixes the level. Rates may differ by activity: take the gradient by labour-market status from the survey and impose it as a scaling that holds the national total fixed.

VerificationFour conditions hold. Every row sums to one less the mortality rate. The implied age profile matches Susenas in all eight years, not only the fitted one. The implied prevalence matches the Global Burden of Disease prevalence, which was not used in the fit. Running the module ten years with no shock leaves the health distribution stable.

CautionDo not report one calibrated pair as if it were measured. Record the range of pairs that fit the age profile about equally well. That range is the sensitivity test in task E.8 and the honest statement of what the data determine.

TASK B.9 Prepare

Construct the wage and effective labour tables

ProcedureTwo small tables. Wages: relativities between occupations from Sakernas, level from the wage bill in the economic database; Susenas has no earnings item. Effective labour: one coefficient per health status, labour per employed person relative to a healthy person, from days of activity lost per illness in the 2013 to 2017 rounds plus inpatient days, which every form records.

VerificationEmployment by occupation times the wage reproduces the wage bill in the economic database.

Why the effective labour coefficient is needed

In both published health applications, better health raised output only by putting more people into work; productivity was unchanged. Where half of employment is informal that omits the main mechanism, because an ill informal worker usually stays employed and works fewer hours. One coefficient per health status captures it.

TASK B.10 Prepare

Compile the assumption register

ProcedureOne table, one row per value that is not measured: the object, the value used, its basis, and the external total it was scaled to. The South African register has eighteen rows; this one will have fewer, because Susenas measures several quantities that were assumed there. Table 8 gives the rows known now.

VerificationAn independent reader can trace every unmeasured value to a stated basis.

TASK B.11 Prepare

Write the module arrays to a model data file

ProcedureWrite each array to its own named header in one binary data file: activities, lagged activities, categories, offers, realised flows, the transition matrix, wages.

VerificationEvery array has the dimensions recorded in task B.1, read off in the data viewer supplied with the modelling software. A transposed dimension gives a model that compiles, solves, and is wrong.

CautionThe module is small: about eight regions, fifteen activities, five ages, two sexes and three health states give about 3,600 category cells. Computing capacity is never the constraint in this project.

Stage B completion gate
  • The activities table reproduces the published population, employment and unemployment totals (B.3).
  • The realised flow table reproduces the observed activity totals after the vacancy loop converges (B.6).
  • Every transition matrix row sums to one less the mortality rate, and the implied age profile matches Susenas in all eight years (B.8).
  • The assumption register is complete and every unmeasured value has a basis (B.10).
  • The data file opens and every array has the dimensions of B.1 (B.11).

8Stage C. Modification of the economic database

Input two mapping files Output a rebuilt and verified database Duration weeks
Governing principle

Never edit the model database directly. Edit the input files and run the pipeline. The pipeline rebuilds trade flows, transport margins and balances, and the verification program then confirms every accounting identity. A direct edit makes every identity the modeller's responsibility with nothing to detect a violation.

TASK D.3 Build Difficult

Implement the category transition equation

ProcedureThe first governing equation: this year's categories equal last year's activities premultiplied by the transition matrix. The activity label is kept; age and health advance; some die.

! Current categories = preceding activities advanced by the transition matrix.
! d_transit is the change in a transition rate. It is the ONLY point
! at which a health policy enters the module.
Equation E_cat
  (all,o,ACTV)(all,a,AGE)(all,g,SEX)(all,d,DST)(all,h,HLT)
  CAT(o,a,g,d,h) * cat(o,a,g,d,h)
    = sum{aa,AGE, sum{hh,HLT,
        ACTL(o,aa,g,d,hh) * TRAN(o,aa,g,d,hh,a,h)
          * [actl(o,aa,g,d,hh) + d_transit(o,aa,g,d,hh,a,h)] }};

VerificationA run with no shock returns zero everywhere. Anything else means the matrix rows do not sum to one less the mortality rate; return to task B.8.

TASK D.4 Build Difficult

Implement the offer equations

ProcedureThe second governing equation, Figure 4 as code. Each category divides its members among destinations by wage and preference weight. Two restrictions: persons outside the labour force offer only to stay there, except for the return flow below; offers go only to occupations of similar skill.

Departure from the published specification

South Africa allowed no return from outside the labour force. In Indonesia most non-participants are women in domestic work and students, and they do return. Allow a return flow with a low offer weight and record the weight in the register (task B.10).

VerificationA one per cent wage rise in one occupation raises offers to it, lowers offers elsewhere, and leaves the total unchanged. The total is the category size, which this equation cannot change.

TASK D.5 Build Difficult

Implement vacancy determination and rationing

ProcedureVacancies equal labour demand less persons who keep their position. Newcomers to an occupation are allocated in proportion to the offers made to it. Those refused enter unemployment; those refused while already unemployed enter long-term unemployment.

VerificationFlows into each occupation sum to industry labour demand for it. This block joins the module to the rest of the model; an inequality here gives a model that solves while the accounts drift.

TASK D.6 Build Difficult

Connect the module to the regional labour market

ProcedureTwo substitutions and no others. (1) The regional labour market equation now sets supply from a wage elasticity; replace its supply side with the sum of accepted offers from the module. (2) Replace the national wage rule with the rule of Figure 5, by occupation and region.

! Present specification, labour market section:
Equation E_flab_i (all,o,OCC)(all,d,DST)
  realwage_i(o,d) = [1/1.1]*xlab_i(o,d) + flabsupA(o,d) + labslack(o);

! Revised: employment in an occupation and region is whatever the
! module's accepted offers deliver. Keep the shifter flabsupA so the
! closure remains usable.

VerificationThe file comparison from task D.1 shows changes only in the labour supply section and the new blocks.

CautionDo not touch industry labour demand, trade or household demand. Reverse any difference the comparison shows elsewhere.

TASK D.7 Build Difficult

Implement the baseline and policy health interfaces

ProcedureTwo interfaces, and they differ. Baseline: the model must follow an externally set path of illness. Add a shift variable on the incidence rate, make it endogenous, and shock new cases to the path; offset the shift in the stay-healthy rate so rows still sum correctly. Policy: the model must receive a deviation from that path, from the pollution calculation. Make the same shift variable exogenous and shock it with the change in incidence by age, sex and region.

VerificationA policy run with a zero deviation reproduces the baseline exactly. Anything else is an error in the stay-healthy offset.

Why this interface is the whole design

Everything upstream of the module, from fuel to emissions to concentrations to exposure–response, reduces to one quantity: the change in incidence by age, sex and region. The module takes that and asks nothing more. It works the same whether the health change comes from air pollution, mining, or revenue spent on clinics. That is what lets three different channels share one mechanism.

TASK D.8 Build

Implement the effective labour and health service extensions

ProcedureTwo additions absent from the published versions. Effective labour: multiply each employed person by the coefficient for their health status from task B.9, so better health raises labour per person as well as persons employed. Health service demand: make demand for the private and public health sectors move with the number in poor health, at a cost per case, so illness avoided is also spending avoided.

VerificationThe database consistency check still passes after a one-year solve.

CautionA shift variable must enter the price and quantity equations together. In the quantity equation alone it solves and then fails the consistency check, because quantities move without a price signal and drift from expenditure.

TASK D.9 Check

Compile, compare and submit for review

ProcedureCompile until no errors remain. Run the file comparison and read every difference. Send the module equations to a reviewer who knows this model family; the project funds that review.

VerificationThree conditions: the model compiles; the comparison shows only intended changes; a run with no shock returns zero everywhere.

Stage D completion gate
  • The model compiles with no errors and the comparison against the reference source shows only intended changes (D.9).
  • A run with no shock returns zero for every variable (D.3, D.9).
  • A policy run with a zero health deviation reproduces the baseline exactly (D.7).
  • The reviewer's comments have been received and addressed (D.9).

10Stage E. Execution of the simulations

Every reported result is the difference between two solutions of the same model, without and with the policy. The baseline is therefore half of every number published.

Input the completed model and database Output one baseline, four policy runs, sensitivity analysis Duration weeks of machine time

TASK E.1 Prepare

Revise the closure for module-determined labour supply

ProcedureRemove the swap recorded in task A.5 that made national labour supply follow the real wage. The module now does that, by occupation and region. Leave the rest of the closure unchanged.

VerificationExogenous variables equal equations. The check tool reports this in seconds.

TASK E.2 Check

Validate all closures and shock files

ProcedureRun the closure and shock check on the baseline closure, each policy closure and each shock file. It reports any shocked variable that is not exogenous and any count that does not add up.

VerificationAll files pass. A run that dies in year four on a closure error costs a day; this check costs a minute.

TASK E.3 Run

Run the baseline simulation

ProcedureSolve year by year from 2023 to 2050 under the existing baseline shocks plus the imposed incidence path of task D.7.

VerificationFour conditions, in order. The macro aggregates follow the paths of task A.4. The health-state shares follow the imposed path. Employment by occupation and region grows smoothly with no discontinuity. The consistency checks pass in every year, not only the first.

CautionIf the macro baseline moved when the module was added, the module is acting in the baseline when it should not. Find the cause before any policy run. A contaminated baseline biases every policy result by the same unknown amount.

TASK E.4 Run

Policy simulation 1: removal of the fossil fuel subsidy

ProcedureA fuel subsidy is a negative commodity tax on fuel. Removing it is a change in that tax by fuel and user, the same instrument the carbon price already uses; no new model structure is needed. Then apply the health consequence: less combustion, lower emissions, lower concentration, fewer new cases, and the change in cases is the shock to the interface of task D.7.

VerificationTwo runs exist, with the health interface at zero and with the health effect applied. Their difference is the health benefit, separated from the direct effect of dearer fuel.

TASK E.5 Run

Policy simulation 2: expansion of mining and smelting

ProcedureShock output of the ore and metal sectors separated in task C.1. The health consequence runs the other way: more dust and water contamination in the extraction regions, so more new cases, concentrated in a few regions.

VerificationThe health loss and the employment gain both appear in the extraction regions and not elsewhere. A health effect spread evenly across the country means the exposure step is using national averages where regional values are required.

TASK E.6 Run

Policy simulation 3: allocation of revenue to health

ProcedureBoth policies raise revenue, the subsidy saved and the mineral tax. Direct it to health spending by region and convert it to a change in incidence or recovery with the elasticity from Work Package 2.2.

VerificationThe revenue spent equals the revenue the model's government accounts gain, both read from the results. Double counting the revenue is a common error and hard for a reader to see.

CautionAsk Work Package 2.2 for the elasticity in the form the module uses: effect on annual incidence or recovery per unit of health spending per person, by region. Any other form needs conversion, and conversion is where errors enter.

TASK E.7 Run

Control simulations without the health channel

ProcedureRun every policy again with the health interface disabled. These runs are required. They are the only way to quantify the health channel, which is the question the project exists to answer.

VerificationThe control run reproduces what a conventional energy study would find. A large divergence means the module affects results through an unintended route.

TASK E.8 Run

Sensitivity analysis

ProcedureFour tests, one for each quantity that is assumed rather than measured (Table 10).

VerificationEvery reported number carries a range. A result that survives all four tests is a finding; one that holds at a single set of values is a description of those values.

Stage E completion gate
  • The baseline reproduces the Stage A macro paths and passes the consistency checks in every year (E.3).
  • Each of the three policies has a run with and a run without the health channel (E.4 to E.7).
  • The revenue spent in simulation 3 equals the revenue gained in the government accounts (E.6).
  • All four sensitivity tests have been run and their ranges recorded (E.8).

11Stage F. Analysis and reporting

A dynamic simulation gives every variable for every year and region. Most of that is not a result. Four tasks reduce it to a reportable form.

Input the solved simulations Output tables, figures and the assumption register Duration weeks

TASK F.1 Prepare

Extract deviations from baseline

ProcedureFor each policy, report the deviation from baseline by year for output, employment by occupation and region, wages, population by health state, and deaths.

VerificationEvery reported series is a deviation, not a level.

CautionWhere baseline and policy share one chart, draw them as two paths from a common origin. No vertical jump at the policy year, and no separate axis tick for it.

TASK F.2 Prepare

Decompose the health and direct effects

ProcedureWith the control runs of task E.7, split each headline number into three parts: the direct effect of the energy or mining policy, the added effect of the health change, and their interaction.

VerificationThe three parts sum to the total. If not, a channel is active in a run where it was meant to be off.

TASK F.3 Prepare

Publish the assumption register with the results

ProcedurePut the register of task B.10 in the published output: each value, its basis, the total it was scaled to, and its sensitivity range from task E.8.

VerificationThe register appears in the paper, not in a file on a machine.

Why the register is what makes the work usable

The South African documentation is still cited fifteen years on, not because the model was unusual, but because every assumed value is in a table with a sentence on its origin. A reader who disputes one value can see exactly which result it affects. That is what lets others build on a module.

TASK F.4 Prepare

Assemble the reproduction package

ProcedureOne directory: the model source and its unmodified reference, the two mapping files, the module data file, every closure and shock file, the command file for each run, and a text file giving the order of execution.

VerificationA colleague not involved in the build reproduces one reported number from the package alone. Any question they must ask is an omission from the text file.

Stage F completion gate
  • Every published number is a deviation from baseline with a sensitivity range (F.1, E.8).
  • The direct, health and interaction parts sum to each total (F.2).
  • The assumption register is in the published output (F.3).
  • An independent colleague has reproduced one number from the package (F.4).

AAppendix A. Glossary

Each term is defined where it first appears; the definitions are collected here.

Activity
The labour-market state during the year: an occupation, unemployment, or non-participation.
Baseline
A solution of the model without the policy. Every result is the difference between a policy solution and the baseline.
Calibration
Choosing an unobserved parameter so that the model reproduces an observed quantity.
Category
A group defined by region, age, sex, health status and the activity of the preceding year.
Closure
The set of variables fixed from outside the model. The same model under a different closure is a different experiment.
Completion gate
The list of conditions at the end of a stage that must all hold before the next stage starts.
Effective labour
Labour supplied per employed person, relative to a person in good health.
Elasticity
The proportional response of one quantity to a proportional change in another.
Emission factor
Pollutant released per unit of fuel burned.
Exposure–response function
The relation between the concentration a population breathes and its rate of disease.
Functional difficulty
The Susenas block of eight graded items on sight, hearing, mobility, manual dexterity, cognition, behaviour, speech and self-care. It defines the health status used here.
Incidence
The annual probability that a healthy person becomes ill.
Incumbent
A person who keeps the position held in the preceding year.
Recovery rate
The annual probability that an ill person returns to good health. Not observed in Indonesia, so calibrated.
Shift variable
A variable added to an equation so that a shock can move it. It must enter the price and quantity equations together.
Source–receptor matrix
How much of a unit emitted in one province reaches the air of each province.
Sticky wage
A wage that adjusts gradually, so that for a period employment is set by demand rather than by the wage.
Swap
Exchanging the exogenous or endogenous status of two variables in a closure.
Transition matrix
The probabilities that a person of given age and health status is in each health status one year later, or has died.
Vacancy
A position not filled by the person who held it in the preceding year.

CAppendix C. The equations of the published specification

Roos (2014, G-241) states the module in forty-two equations. They are reproduced here in full, in the published order and notation, so that the code written in Stage D can be checked line by line against the source. Levels equations are followed by their percentage-change or ordinary-change forms, which is how the model is solved.

Reading the notation. Upper-case names are levels; the same name in lower case is its percentage change; names beginning d_ are ordinary changes. A subscript in parentheses lists the indices. Index o or oo is an activity or category, a and aa are age groups, g is gender, r is race, h and hh are health states, c is a category, q a destination. In the Indonesian module race r becomes region, and the HIV states become the three functional-difficulty states: HIV negative is no difficulty, Stage 1 is some difficulty, and Stage 4, the state that cannot work, is severe difficulty. The elasticity η is σ in Table 2.
