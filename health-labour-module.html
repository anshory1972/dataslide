Before you start

What you are building

The model already knows how much fuel each industry burns in each region. It does not know that burning fuel makes people ill, and that ill people work less. The module is the missing link. It counts people by health state and work status, and it moves them from one year to the next.

What a closure is, in one paragraph

The model has more variables than equations. A closure is the list of variables you hold fixed from outside. Fixing a different set turns the same model into a different experiment. A swap exchanges one fixed variable for another: it makes the first free and the second fixed. The dynamic swaps above are what turns a one-period model into a model that walks forward year by year.

Understand first

Three ideas, three tools

Stage B builds tables of numbers. The tables only make sense if three ideas are clear first. Each tool below lets you move the parameter and watch the answer change. The numbers in the tools are illustrative. They are teaching numbers, not Indonesian data.

Idea 1. The module counts people and moves them

At the start of a year, every working-age person sits in a category: region, age, sex, health state, and what the person did last year. During the year each category makes offers: to stay in the same occupation, to move to another, to be unemployed, or to leave the labour force. Industries decide how many jobs there are. People who held a job last year keep it first; what is left over is shared among the other offers. At the end of the year the transition matrix moves everybody: one year older, some change health state, some die. That gives next year's categories, and the cycle repeats.

Health enters in exactly one place: the transition matrix. Everything else follows.

Teaching tool 1

How an offer responds to wages and to health

A category divides its people between four destinations. The share going to each one depends on the wage there, on a preference weight, and on one elasticity. Poor health lowers the preference for every working destination at once, so the people move towards leaving the labour force.

The starting split matches the published assumptions: about 88 per cent stay, 7 per cent change occupation, 3 per cent go to unemployment. The health slider multiplies the weight on all three working destinations at once.

What to notice. Raise the wage in the other occupation and people move to it, the more so the higher the elasticity. Now lower the health slider instead. Every working destination falls together and the share leaving the labour force rises. That single gradient is what carries the whole result of the study, and Susenas measures it directly. Task B5 is where you estimate it.

Idea 2. A wage that moves slowly turns a supply change into unemployment first

If better health puts more people into the labour market, the wage does not fall at once. It falls by a fraction of the gap between labour demand and labour supply each year. Until it has fallen, the extra people are unemployed. After about five years the wage has done its work and the extra people are employed.

Teaching tool 2

What the sticky wage rule does over ten years

Labour supply rises permanently by one per cent in year 1. The lines show how far employment and the real wage are from the baseline in each year after that.

Employment Real wage

Deviation from baseline, per cent. Illustrative labour demand elasticity of 1.0.

What to notice. The end point does not depend on α. Only the speed does. A slow adjustment means several years of higher unemployment before the gain appears as employment. That transition, not the final number, is what a just transition study is about. Published work uses α between 0.5 and 0.6; you test both in task E8.

Idea 3. You do not need to follow people over time to build a transition matrix

This is the objection you will hear most often, and the answer is the heart of the method. You need two rates: how many healthy people fall ill each year, and how many ill people recover. Indonesia has no survey that follows the same person from year to year. But the share of people who are ill at each age is the accumulated result of both rates acting over a lifetime. Eight annual surveys of that share are enough to recover both rates together.

Teaching tool 3

Find the two rates from the age profile alone

The dots are a target age profile produced by a known pair of rates. Move the two sliders until the line passes through the dots. When it does, you have recovered both rates without following a single person. That is task B8.

Your rates Target profile

Share of people with a functional difficulty, per cent, by single year of age. Illustrative shape, not Susenas data.

What to notice. A high onset rate with a high recovery rate can give almost the same profile as a low onset rate with a low recovery rate, at young ages. The two rates separate at older ages, where the profile bends. That bend is the information. It is also why you must use the whole age range and not only working ages, and why the level is then scaled to an external total from the Global Burden of Disease study.

Why informal work needs its own status

About half of Indonesian workers are informal. When an informal worker becomes ill, the usual response is to work fewer hours, not to stop being employed. If informal workers are counted the same way as formal workers, the health effect on half the workforce cannot be seen at all. This is the main addition to the published specification.

Why this table is better than what South Africa had

Roos could not observe health status and work status for the same person. She had to take national health prevalence from an outside model and spread it across occupations using assumed percentages. Susenas records both for the same person, in the same survey, with region and age. The one table you build here replaces her hardest and least defensible step.

TASK B6

Turn offers into actual flows by the vacancy loop

DoJobs are limited, so most offers are refused. Work out how many jobs are open, then share them out. The calculation is circular, so it is solved by repeating it until the numbers stop moving.

  1. Start by assuming a share of workers is dismissed, three per cent unless Sakernas tells you otherwise.
  2. Count the people who keep their job from last year.
  3. Vacancies are the jobs the industries want, minus the people who kept theirs.
  4. Share the vacancies among the other offers, in proportion to those offers.
  5. Recompute who kept a job now that some have moved, and repeat from step 2.

CheckTwo conditions. The loop must stop moving, and the column totals of the flows table must reproduce the observed employment, unemployment and non-participation totals from B3. If the loop does not settle, the offer strengths in B5 are inconsistent with the number of jobs.

TASK B8

Build the health part of the transition matrix by calibration

This is the hardest task on the page. Teaching tool 3 above is the same problem in miniature. Work through it before starting.

DoIn four steps.

  1. Shape of onset. Take new cases by cause, age and sex from the Global Burden of Disease study for Indonesia. Map the causes onto the three difficulty states. Respiratory and cardiovascular disease matter most here, because they are the causes that air pollution changes.
  2. Recovery is unknown. Nothing measures it directly. Treat it as the parameter to be found.
  3. Calibrate. Choose recovery, and adjust onset, so that the share of people with a difficulty at each age that the module implies matches what Susenas shows, in each of the eight years from 2018 to 2025.
  4. Scale the level. Multiply both rates by a common factor so that the national number of cases by cause matches the Global Burden of Disease total. This is the step that anchors the level; the calibration only fixed the shape.

DoAllow the rates to differ by activity. Roos made progression slower for skilled workers by assumption. You can take the gradient from the survey instead: compute how the share with difficulty varies by work status at each age, and impose that as a scaling, holding the national total fixed.

CheckFour checks, all of them cheap.

  • Every row of the matrix sums to one minus the death rate. This is arithmetic, not judgement.
  • The implied age profile matches Susenas in all eight years, not just the year you fitted.
  • The implied prevalence matches the Global Burden of Disease prevalence, which you did not use in the fitting.
  • Running the module forward for ten years with no shock leaves the health distribution stable. A drift means onset and recovery are not consistent with each other.

Watch outDo not report a single calibrated pair of rates as if it were measured. Record the range of pairs that fit the age profile about equally well. That range becomes a sensitivity test in task E8, and it is the honest statement of what the data can and cannot tell you.

Why effective labour is worth the trouble

In both published health studies, better health raised output only by putting more people into work. Nobody became more productive. In Indonesia, where half the workforce is informal, that leaves out the main effect: an ill informal worker usually keeps working and works fewer hours. One number per health state repairs it.

Add the equation that carries people from one year to the next

DoThis is the first of the three equations. This year's categories are last year's activities multiplied by the transition matrix. People keep their activity label; only age and health change, and some die.

! Categories this year = last year's activities moved by the transition matrix.
! d_transit is the change in a transition rate.
! It is the ONE place a health policy enters the module.
Equation E_cat
  (all,o,ACTV)(all,a,AGE)(all,g,SEX)(all,d,DST)(all,h,HLT)
  CAT(o,a,g,d,h) * cat(o,a,g,d,h)
    = sum{aa,AGE, sum{hh,HLT,
        ACTL(o,aa,g,d,hh) * TRAN(o,aa,g,d,hh,a,h)
          * [actl(o,aa,g,d,hh) + d_transit(o,aa,g,d,hh,a,h)] }};

CheckRun the model once with no shock at all. Every result must be zero. If anything moves, the transition matrix rows do not sum to one minus the death rate, and you must go back to task B8.

TASK D4

Add the offer equations

DoThe second equation. Each category divides its people between destinations according to the wage on offer and a preference weight. This is teaching tool 1, written as code.

Two details that are easy to miss. People who are out of the labour force offer only to stay there, unless you add the return flow described below. And offers are restricted to occupations of similar skill, so a labourer does not offer to be a manager.

An addition the published version does not have

In the South African module, nobody returns from outside the labour force. In Indonesia most people outside the labour force are women in home duties and students, and they do return. Allow the return flow, with a low offer weight. Write the weight in the register from task B10.

CheckWith a one per cent rise in one occupation's wage and nothing else, offers to that occupation should rise and offers elsewhere should fall, and the total across destinations should not change at all. The total is the category size, which this equation cannot alter.

TASK D5

Add vacancies and the sharing out of jobs

DoVacancies are the jobs industries want minus the people who kept the job they held. Non-incumbent flows into an occupation are shared in proportion to the offers made to it. Those who fail to get a job go to unemployment, and those who fail from unemployment go to long-term unemployment.

CheckSum the flows into each occupation and compare with the labour demand from the industries. They must be equal. This is the equation block that connects the module to the rest of the model, and an inequality here is the error that produces a model which solves but drifts.

TASK D6

Connect the module where the old labour supply used to be

DoTwo replacements, and nothing else.

  1. The regional labour market equation currently sets labour supply from a wage elasticity. Replace its supply side with the sum of accepted offers from the module.
  2. The national wage adjustment rule is replaced by the sticky wage rule from teaching tool 2, written by occupation and by region rather than nationally.
! What is there now, in the labour market section:
Equation E_flab_i (all,o,OCC)(all,d,DST)
  realwage_i(o,d) = [1/1.1]*xlab_i(o,d) + flabsupA(o,d) + labslack(o);

! What it becomes: employment in an occupation and region is
! whatever the module's accepted offers deliver.
! Keep the shifter flabsupA so the closure can still be used.

Watch outDo not touch industry labour demand, trade, or household demand. If your file comparison from D1 shows changes anywhere except the labour supply section and your new blocks, undo them.

TASK D7

Add the two health hooks

DoThe module needs two ways to receive health information, and they are different.

  • In the baseline. You want the model to follow a path of illness that you decide from outside. Add a shift variable on the onset rate, make it endogenous, and shock the number of new cases to the path you want. Offset the shift in the stay-healthy rate so that the rows of the matrix still sum correctly.
  • In a policy run. You want a deviation from that baseline, coming from the pollution calculation. The same shift variable is now exogenous, and you shock it directly with the change in new cases by age, sex and region.

CheckShock the deviation by zero in a policy run. Every result must be zero, and the run must reproduce the baseline exactly. If it does not, the offsetting in the stay-healthy rate is wrong.

Why this hook is the whole design

Everything upstream of the module, the fuel, the emissions, the concentrations, the exposure–response curves, ends as one thing: a change in the number of new cases by age, sex and region. The module receives that and asks no further questions. It works exactly the same way whether the health change came from air pollution, from mining, or from spending the revenue on clinics. That is what lets three very different channels share one piece of machinery.

TASK D8

Add the two extensions

DoTwo small additions that the published versions do not have.

  • Effective labour. Multiply each employed person by the effective-labour number for their health state from task B9, so that healthier workers supply more labour and not only more people.
  • Health service demand. Make demand for the private and government health sectors move with the number of people in poor health, at a cost per case. Then illness avoided is also spending avoided, which is a real part of the benefit.

Watch outA shift variable must enter the price equation and the quantity equation together. A version that enters only the quantity equation will solve, and will then fail the database check, because quantities that move without a price signal drift away from purchases.

TASK D9

Compile, compare and get the code reviewed

DoCompile until there are no errors. Then run the file comparison from D1 and read every difference. Then send the module equations to a reviewer who knows this family of models. The Wellcome project pays for that review; use it.

CheckThree things before you call Stage D finished: the model compiles, the comparison shows only intended changes, and a run with no shock gives exactly zero everywhere.

Stage E

Run the simulations

Every result in this kind of study is a difference between two runs of the same model: one without the policy, one with it. So the baseline is not a warm-up. It is half of every number you will publish.

Input the finished model and database Output one baseline, four policy runs, sensitivity Effort weeks of machine time

TASK E1

Change the closure so the module supplies the labour

DoRemove the swap you wrote down in task A5, the one that made national labour supply follow the real wage. The module now does that job, by occupation and region. Everything else in the closure stays as it was.

CheckCount the exogenous variables. The number must equal the number of equations. The checking tool does this for you and takes seconds.

TASK E2

Check every closure and every shock file before running anything

DoRun the closure and shock check on the baseline closure, on each policy closure, and on each shock file. It reads the model, applies each closure and reports any variable that is shocked but not exogenous, or any count that does not add up.

CheckAll files pass. An overnight run that dies at year 4 on a closure error costs a day. This check costs a minute.

TASK E3

Run the baseline to 2050 with the health path imposed

DoRun the model year by year from 2023 to 2050 with the existing baseline shocks, plus your imposed path of new cases from task D7.

CheckFour things in the baseline, in this order.

  • The economic aggregates still follow the paths they followed in task A4. Adding a module should not change the baseline economy.
  • The share of people in each health state follows the path you imposed.
  • Employment by occupation and region grows at a sensible rate, with no cliff in any year.
  • The database checks pass in every year, not only the first.

Watch outIf the economic baseline moved when you added the module, the module is doing something in the baseline that it should not. Find it before going any further. A contaminated baseline makes every policy result wrong by the same unknown amount.

TASK E4

Policy run 1: remove the fossil fuel subsidy

DoA fuel subsidy is a negative tax on fuel purchases. Removing it is a change in that tax, by fuel and by user. This is the same instrument the carbon tax already uses, so the model needs nothing new for it.

Then feed the health consequence in: less fuel burned gives lower emissions, lower emissions give lower concentrations, lower concentrations give fewer new cases, and the change in new cases is the shock to the module hook from task D7.

CheckRun it once with the health hook shocked to zero, and once with the health effect in. The difference between those two runs is the health benefit, cleanly separated from the direct effect of dearer fuel.

TASK E5

Policy run 2: expand mining and smelting

DoShock the output of the ore and metal sectors you separated out in task C1. The health consequence here runs the other way: more dust and more water contamination in the mining regions, so more new cases, concentrated in a few regions.

CheckThe regional pattern is the test. The health loss should appear in the mining regions and not elsewhere, and the employment gain should appear in the same regions. If the health effect is spread evenly across the country, your exposure step is using national averages where it should be using regional ones.

TASK E6

Policy run 3: spend the revenue on health

DoBoth policies raise revenue: the subsidy saved, and the tax on minerals. Direct that revenue to health spending by region, and convert it into fewer new cases or faster recovery using the elasticity that Work Package 2.2 provides.

Watch outAsk Work Package 2.2 for the elasticity in the form you can use: the effect on annual new cases or on recovery, per rupiah of health spending per person, by region. An elasticity expressed any other way will have to be converted, and the conversion is where errors hide.

CheckThe revenue spent here must be the same revenue the government accounts of the model gained. Read both from the results and confirm they match. A benefit financed twice is the easiest mistake to make and the hardest for a reader to spot.

TASK E7

Run the control case with no health channel at all

DoRun every policy again with the health hook switched off. This is not a spare run. It is the only way to say how much of your result comes from health, which is the question the project exists to answer.

CheckThe control run should reproduce what a conventional energy study would find. If it differs a lot, your module is affecting the results through a route you have not intended.

TASK E8

Run the sensitivity tests, and report them with the results

DoFour tests, chosen because each one is a number you assumed rather than measured.

TestRangeWhat it tells you
Wage adjustment speed0.5 and 0.6How long the transition lasts
Offer elasticity1 to 3, around 2How easily people change occupation
Onset and recovery pairThe range that fits the age profile, from task B8How much the health result depends on a rate you could not observe
Offer strength by healthPlus and minus a quarterHow much rests on the participation gradient

CheckEvery published number gets a range beside it. A result that survives all four tests is a finding. A result that only holds at one set of assumed values is a description of those assumptions.

Stage F

Report the results

The output of a dynamic model is large: every variable, every year, every region. Most of it is not a result. These four tasks turn the output into something a reader can use.

Input the solved simulations Output tables, charts and the register Effort weeks

TASK F1

Extract the differences from baseline, not the levels

DoFor each policy run, report the difference from the baseline, year by year. Report it for output, employment by occupation and region, wages, the number of people in each health state, and deaths.

Watch outWhen you draw the baseline and the policy as two paths on one chart, draw them as two lines from a common start. Do not draw a vertical jump at the policy year, and do not give the policy its own tick on the axis.

TASK F2

Separate the health effect from the direct effect

DoUse the control runs from task E7. Every headline number splits into three parts: what the energy or mining policy does directly, what the health change adds, and what the two do together.

CheckThe three parts must add up to the total. If they do not, a channel is active in a run where you thought it was switched off.

TASK F3

Publish the assumption register next to the results

DoPut the register from task B10 in the paper, not in a file on your machine. Every assumed number, its value, its basis, the total it was scaled to, and the sensitivity range from task E8.

Why this is the deliverable that makes the work usable

The South African documentation is still cited fifteen years on, not because the model was unusual, but because every assumed number is in a table with a sentence saying where it came from. A reader who disagrees with one of them can see exactly which result to distrust. That is what makes a module something other people can build on.

TASK F4

Package the work so someone else can run it

DoOne folder containing: the model file and the original it was copied from, the two mapping files, the module data file, every closure and shock file, the command files for each run, and a plain text file saying what to run and in what order.

CheckGive the folder to a colleague who was not involved and ask them to reproduce one number. Whatever they have to ask you is what is missing from the text file.

Reference

Words used on this page

Each term is explained once where it first appears. They are collected here so you do not have to search for them.

Baseline
A run of the model without the policy. Every result is the difference between a policy run and the baseline.
Category
A group of people defined by region, age, sex, health state, and what they did last year.
Activity
What a person does during the year: an occupation, unemployment, or being outside the labour force.
Transition matrix
A table of probabilities. For a person of a given age and health state, it gives the chance of being in each health state one year later, or of having died.
Onset rate
The chance that a healthy person becomes ill during a year.
Recovery rate
The chance that an ill person becomes healthy during a year. Not measured in Indonesia, so it is calibrated.
Vacancy
A job that the person who held it last year does not fill this year.
Incumbent
A person who keeps the job they held last year.
Sticky wage
A wage that moves only slowly, so for several years the number of jobs is set by demand rather than by the wage.
Elasticity
How strongly one thing responds to another, measured as a percentage change divided by a percentage change.
Closure
The list of variables held fixed from outside. The same model with a different closure is a different experiment.
Swap
Exchanging one fixed variable for another: the first becomes free, the second becomes fixed.
Shift variable
A variable added to an equation so that a shock can move that equation. It must be added to the price and the quantity equation together.
Calibration
Choosing a number you cannot observe so that the model reproduces something you can observe.
Emission factor
How much of a pollutant is released per unit of fuel burned.
Source–receptor matrix
A table saying how much of a tonne emitted in one province reaches the air of another.
Exposure–response curve
The relation between the pollution a population breathes and its rate of disease.
Functional difficulty
The Susenas block of eight graded questions on seeing, hearing, walking, using the hands, remembering, behaviour, speaking and self-care. It defines the health state used here.

Sources. The module specification and its database recipe are Roos (2014, working paper G-241) and Roos (2013, working paper G-235), Centre of Policy Studies. The applied versions are Roos and Giesecke (2014) for South Africa and Verikios, Dixon, Rimmer and Harris (2013) for Australia. The IndoTERM database and its build pipeline are documented in Horridge, Roos, Yusuf and Suryana (2021). The host model is the GMMI energy version of IndoTERM. Household data are the BPS Susenas March core questionnaires and Sakernas; health rates are from the Global Burden of Disease study.

The three teaching tools use illustrative numbers. They show how the mechanism behaves, not what Indonesian data say. The target profile in tool 3 is generated from a known pair of rates so that the exercise has an answer.
