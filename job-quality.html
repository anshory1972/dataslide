The Quality of Indonesia’s New Jobs

Arief Anshory Yusuf

Employment has grown every year since the pandemic, and the headline unemployment rate keeps falling. What that employment is made of has been getting worse. Four measures — hours, formal status, the pay of the sectors taking on workers, and the real wage — all point the same way, and one of them is stark enough to need no argument at all.

August 2025

Indonesia added 1.9 million jobs last year. 1.7 million of them were part-time

Employment keeps growing. What it is made of has been getting worse since 2019, and in the year to August 2025 almost none of the additional work was full-time.

IHow this is measured

Four cuts of the same survey, one rule about which rounds may be compared, and one warning about a table that disagrees with itself.

Method

Four measures of quality, one rule about rounds

“More jobs” and “better jobs” are different claims. These four cuts of the same Sakernas can be read against each other.

Hours

Full-time means 35 hours a week or more, plus those temporarily away who worked zero hours. That convention reproduces the published Pekerja Penuh series to two decimals in every year.

Careful

BPS publishes two different totals for employment in August 2018 and August 2019. Var 529, 1006 and 1971 give 126.28m and 128.76m; var 2335 gives 124.00m and 126.52m. Every other year from 2015 to 2025 agrees across all four tables. The gap is about 2.2 million, so “jobs created since 2019” is either 17.8 or 20.0 million depending on the table — a 13% difference. This page uses each table internally consistently and never mixes them in one calculation.

IIHours

The measure that needs no interpretation: how much of the new work is full-time.

Hours

The full-time share has not recovered, and last year it fell again

71.0% of Indonesians in work were full-time in 2019. In 2025 it was 67.3%. The gap is about five million people who would be working full-time on the old composition and are not.

2022 is the year the recovery actually happened: 8.4 million net new full-time jobs. Nothing since has come close. In 2023 full-time work was 83% of the net increase, in 2024 43%, and in 2025 10%.

Source & method
Var 1006, August rounds. Full-time is the sum of the 35–44, 45–54, 55–59, 60–74 and 75+ hour bands plus the zero-hour band, which holds workers temporarily away from a job they hold. Checked against the published Pekerja Penuh percentages: 70.12 in 2015, 63.85 in 2020, 67.32 in 2025.

IIIFormal employment

The level, the share, and the only test that decides whether formality rises or falls.

Formal employment

Formal work is growing again, and is still far below the path it was on

There were 61.8 million formal workers in August 2025, up from 50.8 million at the bottom. But formal work is a smaller share of all work than it was in 2017, and the 2019 rate has not been regained.

The projection is the 2017–2019 linear trend in formal employment extended to 2025; on a compound trend of 3.79% a year the shortfall is 8.2 million instead of 6.2 million. DEN's own November 2025 brief projects the employee series alone and finds a shortfall of 8.1 million. All three say the same thing at different magnitudes.

Formal employment

Whether formality rises depends on the jobs at the margin, not the stock

The formal share of employment can only rise if the formal share of the new jobs is higher than the share of the existing ones. On that test Indonesia has passed every year since 2020. The trouble is the speed.

In 2019, 99.5% of the net new jobs were formal and the share jumped more than a point. Since 2021 the marginal rate has run between 45% and 76% against a stock in the low forties, so formality has climbed back every year. But the pace is everything. At the 2024 combination — 4.8 million new jobs, 75.8% of them formal — Indonesia would regain its 2019 formal share of 44.3% by 2028. At the 2025 combination — 1.9 million new jobs, 53.9% formal — it would take until 2042. The direction is right; one bad year moves the arrival date by fourteen years.

Source & method
Formal share of the net increase in employment, var 2335. 2020 is off the scale: employment grew by 1.9 million while formal employment fell by 5.2 million, so the marginal rate was −271%.

Who

Formality rose for the least educated and fell for graduates

12.2 million of the 18.1 million net new jobs since 2020 went to people whose highest qualification is senior secondary. Degree and diploma holders are the only groups whose formal share went down.

This is not a story of educated workers being pushed into informal work in large numbers — 75% of the new jobs taken by graduates were formal. It is that the formal share within the graduate group slipped 1.6 points and within the diploma group 2.2 points, while the aggregate share rose because employment shifted toward groups that start from a much lower base.

Source & method
Var 1126, status by highest education completed, August 2020 and August 2025. This variable starts in 2020, so 2019 is not available on this cut. Primary and diploma show falling total employment, which is a composition effect as cohorts move up the education ladder.

Where

Jawa Barat added 2.6 million jobs and 128,000 formal ones

Formality fell in 25 of the 32 provinces whose boundaries did not change. The falls are concentrated in the industrial and resource provinces.

Five provinces have fewer formal workers in 2025 than in 2019 in absolute terms: Banten (−109,000), DKI Jakarta (−61,000), Aceh (−32,000), DI Yogyakarta (−19,000) and Kepulauan Bangka Belitung (−3,000). Jakarta added 291,000 jobs over the six years and lost 61,000 formal ones.

Source & method
Var 2335, province rows, August 2019 and August 2025. Papua and Papua Barat are excluded because their territory was divided in 2022 and the two years do not describe the same area; the four new Papua provinces have no 2019 observation.

IVWhat the work pays

The sectors doing the hiring, and what has happened to the average packet.

What the jobs pay

Two thirds of the jobs added since 2019 are in the three lowest-paying sectors

Agriculture, trade, and accommodation and food took 69% of the net increase in employment between 2019 and 2025. They are also, with one exception, the lowest-paid sectors in the country.

Weighting each sector by the jobs it actually added, the average new job since 2019 sits in a sector paying Rp 2.94 million a month, against a national average of Rp 3.33 million. Sectors paying below the national average took 87% of the net increase. Finance and insurance, the second best paid, is the only sector that shed workers.

Source & method
Employment by sector from var 1971 (August, total across education levels); average monthly wage by sector from var 1521, August 2025. The two use the same 17-sector classification, so the join is exact.

Pay

The average wage buys less than it did in 2017

More work, of worse composition, at a real wage that has gone nowhere for eight years.

The deflator matters and should always be named. Against the consumer price index the average wage is 2.0% lower than in 2017. Against the poverty-line basket, which has been rising far faster than the CPI, the fall is considerably larger.

Source & method
Var 1521 (Rata-Rata, August) deflated by a national CPI chained from var 1 month-on-month inflation and rebased to August 2025. The Sakernas wage series breaks in mid-2016 — nominal wages jump 17.1% between February and August 2016 against 4–8% in every other year — so the comparable run starts in 2017.

Read the 2021–2025 row carefully. Measured from the COVID trough, 93% of the net new jobs were full-time and 56% formal, and the picture looks excellent. That is what recovering from a hole looks like, not progress. 2019 is the defensible base, because 2020 destroyed 9.4 million full-time and 5.2 million formal jobs. Say which base you are using, out loud, before anyone asks.

What this does not show
