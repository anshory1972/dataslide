Predicting Welfare: What a Proxy Means Test Teaches About Regression

Arief Anshory Yusuf

A proxy means test is an ordinary linear regression asked to do an unusual job. It is never used to explain anything — only to guess a number the analyst cannot observe, for a household the analyst has never met. That single change of purpose reorganises every methodological choice: which variables belong, what functional form to use, what fixed effects are for, and above all what counts as evidence that the model works. This lecture follows one real model — estimated on 1.02 million Indonesian households and tested on 343,460 more — and uses it to make those choices concrete.

IThe question decides the method

Almost everything that feels strange about a targeting model follows from one fact: we are predicting, not explaining. Get that straight and the rest of the course falls into place.

Figure 1

Two questions, two econometrics

The same regression command answers both. The standards of evidence are almost opposites.

1

Causal — why

Goal: an unbiased β on one variable of interest.

Threats: omitted variables, simultaneity, selection.

You worry about: identification, exogeneity, the exclusion restriction.

A control is good if it closes a back-door path. Adding a bad control is a disaster even if R² rises.

Inference
2

Predictive — how much

Goal: a small ŷ error on data you have never seen.

Threats: overfitting, distribution shift, leakage.

You worry about: out-of-sample loss, variance, stability over time.

A variable is good if it lowers test error. Whether it causes welfare is irrelevant — and asking is a category error.

Prediction

A proxy means test lives entirely in column 2. Nobody claims a motorcycle causes consumption. The claim is only that households owning one tend to spend more, and that this regularity is stable enough to act on. Students trained only on causal inference routinely mis-read PMT coefficients as effects. They are not effects. They are weights.

Source & method
The distinction follows Shmueli (2010), To Explain or to Predict?, and Kleinberg, Ludwig, Mullainathan & Obermeyer (2015) on “prediction policy problems”. Targeting is their canonical example: the policy payoff depends on ŷ alone, with no causal parameter anywhere in the decision.

Figure 2

What a proxy means test actually is

One regression, estimated on a survey where welfare is observed, then applied to households where it is not.

Step 1 — estimate, on a survey with full consumption data:

ln yi  =  xi′β  +  αd(i)  +  εi

y = real per-capita expenditure  ·  x = cheap observable proxies  ·  αd = district effect

Step 2 — predict, for a household with no consumption data:

ln ŷj  =  xj′b  +  ad(j)

b and ad are the estimates of β and αd from Step 1. Note what is missing: no ε. The residual is exactly the part of welfare the proxies cannot see, and it does not vanish — we simply have no estimate of it for household j.

That omitted ε is the whole problem of targeting. Its standard deviation in this model is 0.365 in logs — roughly ±44% / −31% in rupiah. Every error rate later in this lecture is a consequence of that one number.

Source & method
Notation follows the standard PMT set-up in Grosh, Leite, Wai-Poi & Tesliuc (2022), Revisiting Targeting in Social Assistance, World Bank. Root MSE from the national parsimonious model with district fixed effects, Susenas 2022–2024, N = 1,024,865.

IISpecification

Functional form, zeros, and fixed effects — three choices that look technical, each with a consequence you can measure.

Figure 4

Why logs, and why squares

Three reasons, none of them decoration.

1

Skew

Telecom spending has a median of Rp 35,667 and a maximum of Rp 10.3 million — roughly 290× the median. Electricity: median 33 kWh, max 3,704. In levels, a handful of households would dominate the fit. Logs pull the tail in.

2

Elasticity

With both sides in logs the coefficient is an elasticity — a percentage response to a percentage change. That is the natural scale for consumption, and it makes coefficients comparable across variables measured in kWh, m² and rupiah.

3

Curvature

The squared terms let the relationship bend. For telecom the linear term is −0.147 and the quadratic +0.021 — a U-shape whose rising arm is where almost every household sits. The marginal effect at the median is +0.30, not −0.147.

The teaching point students miss. With a quadratic in the model you cannot read the coefficient as the effect. ∂ln y / ∂ln t = β5 + 2β6 ln t, which depends on where you evaluate it. A negative β5 alongside a positive β6 is not a sign error — it is a curve, and the sign of the slope changes at ln t = −β5/2β6.

Source & method
Distributional figures from Susenas March 2025, household-weighted (wert), n = 343,460. Coefficients from the national parsimonious model with district fixed effects, trained on 2022–2024.

Figure 5

The zero problem, and the price of the usual fix

ln(0) is undefined. In this data that is not a corner case: it is 6.5% of households for telecom and 21–25% for its components.

ln(x + 0.1)    for electricity, telecom, floor area
ln(n)          for household size — no offset, because n ≥ 1 always

The offset keeps zero-spending households in the sample instead of dropping them. But it is not free, and the cost is worth stating plainly to students:

What it costs

The constant is arbitrary. Choosing 0.1 rather than 1 or 0.01 changes where the zeros land on the log scale — ln(0.1) = −2.30 versus ln(1) = 0. Since the specification is quadratic, that relocation is squared, so the fit near zero is sensitive to a number nobody estimated.

The alternatives

A zero dummy plus log of the positive part; inverse hyperbolic sine; or a two-part model. Each is defensible. This pipeline chose the offset for simplicity — a reasonable call, but one that should be declared rather than buried, because it is a researcher degree of freedom.

A practical corollary for anyone re-implementing the model: the offset must be reproduced exactly, including the asymmetry that household size does not get one. Applying ln(n + 0.1) by mistake changes every prediction.

Source & method
Zero shares from Susenas March 2025, household-weighted: 24.6% of households report zero phone credit, 21.6% zero internet package, but only 6.5% zero for the combined telecom variable — the two components substitute for one another. On alternatives to the log offset see Bellemare & Wichman (2020) on the inverse hyperbolic sine, and Chen & Roth (2024) on log-like transformations with zeros.

Figure 6

What 513 dummies absorb

The district effect is not a question asked of the household. It is the district the household already lives in — free information, and it buys a lot.

What is the αd actually soaking up? Everything that is common to a district and correlated with welfare but absent from the eight predictors: local price levels the deflator missed, labour-market conditions, remoteness, public-service quality, agro-ecology. In a causal paper you would agonise over whether these belong. Here the question does not arise — if it lowers test error, it belongs.

But there is a catch that only appears out of sample, and it is the subject of Figure 15. A fixed effect estimated on 2022–2024 is applied to 2025 as a constant. If the district's position shifted in the meantime, the correction is confidently wrong — and it is wrong for every household in that district at once.

Source & method
Both models estimated by OLS on pooled Susenas 2022–2024, N = 1,024,865. The base district is Kab. Simeulue (code 1101); all other district effects are measured relative to it.

IIIChoosing among models

Thirty-nine variables or eight? The answer is not what most students — or most practitioners — expect.

Figure 8

Two candidate variable sets

Both are defensible a priori. They differ in kind, not just in count — and that turns out to be the whole story.

39

The conventional set

Demographics by age band and sex; education counts; employment status by sector; housing material for floor, wall, roof; water source; lighting; cooking fuel; toilet type; septic type; fourteen asset dummies; marital status.

Almost every variable is categorical or a count. Nothing measures a quantity consumed.

Baseline
8

The parsimonious set

Household size · electricity kWh per capita · telecom spending per capita · floor area · electricity meter capacity · car · computer · land.

Two of the eight are continuous consumption quantities — near-measures of the thing being predicted, not proxies for it.

Parsimonious

The instinct in applied work is that more information cannot hurt: with enough observations, irrelevant regressors merely get small coefficients. That instinct is right about bias and wrong about variance — and out of sample, variance is what you pay for.

Source & method
Baseline set as specified in do file 2 ($predictors, 39 fields). Parsimonious set is $predictors_7, 8 underlying variables entering as 15 regression terms.

Figure 10

The out-of-sample design

Held-out data is not a formality here. Targeting models are always applied to a year they were not estimated on, so the test must imitate that.

Train  Susenas March 2022, 2023, 2024  —  1,024,865 households

Test   Susenas March 2025  —  343,460 households, never seen in estimation

Deciles for the test year are assigned from the 2025 cross-section alone, so the evaluation reflects actual 2025 welfare ranks, not ranks blended with training years.

Three properties of this design deserve comment, because each is a decision students should be able to defend:

Temporal, not random

A random 80/20 split would leak: households from the same year, same enumeration area, share shocks. Splitting by year tests what the model is actually asked to do — extrapolate forward.

Ranks re-computed

Predicted deciles come from ranking predicted welfare in the test year. This is what an implementing agency would do, and it avoids importing training-period thresholds into a different price environment.

Weighted throughout

Errors are population-weighted. An unweighted rate would over-represent small sampled households and understate error among the populous poor.

Source & method
Pipeline do files 2–4. Predicted deciles assigned by weighted percentile ranking (xtile ... [fw=int(weind)], nq(10)) on the 2025 predictions.

IVEvaluating a prediction

R² is the wrong scoreboard for a targeting model. What replaces it depends on a policy choice the econometrician does not get to make alone.

Figure 11

R² answers the wrong question

A model can fit well on average and still put the wrong households in the programme. Those are different objectives, and only one of them pays benefits.

What R² measures

Squared error over the whole distribution. It rewards getting the rich approximately right just as much as getting the near-threshold poor exactly right.

What targeting needs

Correct classification at a cut-off. A household at the 19th percentile predicted at the 21st is a policy failure; a billionaire predicted as a millionaire is not.

Put the two scales side by side. Root MSE is 0.365 in logs. Decile boundaries in this distribution are only 0.10 to 0.16 log points apart. The prediction error therefore spans roughly three deciles. A model can have a perfectly respectable R² of 0.66 and still be unable to distinguish decile 3 from decile 6 for an individual household. Both statements are true simultaneously, and only the second one matters at the counter.

Source & method
Root MSE from the national parsimonious + FE model. Decile boundary spacing computed from the realised distribution of predicted log expenditure on the 2025 test data, 343,460 households.

Figure 12

Inclusion and exclusion error

Two rates, two denominators. Confusing them is the single most common error in reading targeting evaluations.

Inclusion error — denominator is those selected

IE = 1 − P( truly poor | selected by the PMT )

Exclusion error — denominator is those who qualify

EE = 1 − P( selected by the PMT | truly poor )

When the programme selects exactly the same population share as the true criterion — the bottom 20% by prediction against the bottom 20% by actual welfare — the two rates are equal by construction. That is an arithmetic identity, not a finding, and it is why this pipeline reports IE20 = EE20 = 39.7%.

A third framing that reconciles them. The same model can be described as: 39.7% inclusion error among those selected, or 15.9% of the whole population placed on the wrong side of the bottom-20% line. Same model, same data, different denominators. When a policy note says “misclassification” without saying of what, it has not said anything.

Source & method
Definitions follow do file 4. Both rates computed with individual sampling weights (weind) on the 2025 test data.

Compare the first two rows before the third. XGBoost and OLS on the same 39 variables perform almost identically — 74.9% versus 74.8% wrong-decile in Figure 13. The flexible learner extracted essentially nothing that the linear model missed. Then compare row three: eight variables, plain OLS, and the poorest-decile hit rate jumps nine points.

So the gap is not the algorithm. It is the information. The 39-variable set contains no electricity consumption and no telecom spending. No amount of functional flexibility recovers a variable that was never collected.

The general lesson for applied work: when a flexible learner fails to beat OLS, that is evidence about the features, not the estimator. Reach for better measurement before reaching for a better model class.

VHeterogeneity

One national model, thirty-eight provincial ones, or five hundred and fourteen district ones? The bias–variance trade-off, with real numbers attached.

Figure 15

One model or many? The bias–variance trade-off, made concrete

Letting coefficients vary by region reduces bias and raises variance. The optimum is interior — and here you can see exactly where it sits.

LevelModels fittedObs per modelError at bottom 20%Papua: off by ≥2 deciles

The textbook shape, observed. Moving from one model to thirty-eight helps: provincial slopes capture real regional differences in how electricity and telecom map to welfare. Moving from thirty-eight to five hundred and fourteen does not help — and in the region that needs it most, it actively hurts. With only ~2,000 observations per district, sampling noise in the estimated coefficients outweighs the extra flexibility.

Province is the interior optimum, and it was not obvious in advance. The in-sample fit keeps improving as you disaggregate — district R² reaches 0.821 in Kota Surabaya — while out-of-sample accuracy stops improving. That divergence between in-sample and out-of-sample is precisely the phenomenon the trade-off names.

Provincial in-sample Adj. R² ranges from 0.570 in Bangka Belitung to 0.792 in DKI Jakarta; district-level from 0.379 to 0.821. The widening spread as you disaggregate is variance appearing, not information.

Source & method
Do file 3 estimates all three levels on identical specifications and training data. When the parsimonious model is run within a single district, all district dummies become collinear and are dropped, so the district model is 15 terms plus a constant.

The diagnostic. Across Papua, the correlation between a district's fixed effect and its prediction error is +0.766 with the fixed effects and −0.826 without them. That near-mirror image is informative: the effects capture something real — drop them and the same districts are under-predicted by as much — but they overshoot. The truth sits between the two columns.

And the obvious remedy is the wrong one. Shrinkage (xtreg, re) is the standard fix for noisy group effects, and here it does almost nothing: with ~2,000 households per district the implied shrinkage factor is 0.997. The effects are precisely estimated. What fails is that they do not persist from 2022–24 into 2025 — temporal instability, not sampling noise. Different diagnosis, different cure.

In Yalimo, 35.9% of the population truly belongs in the national bottom 20%; the fixed-effects model places 0.4% there. Aggregate statistics conceal this completely — Papua-wide the model looks fine, because over- and under-prediction across districts cancel.

Source & method
Own calculation on the 2025 test data. Shrinkage factor from xtreg, re: σu = 0.145, σe = 0.365, mean 1,994 households per district, giving λ = σu²/(σu² + σe²/n) ≈ 0.997. Regressing the random-effects predictions on the fixed-effects estimates gives a slope of 0.9963 with R² = 1.000.

VIWhat the model cannot see

Every error rate quoted so far assumes the dependent variable is correct. It is not, and that changes how the numbers should be read.

Figure 17

Measurement error — in the outcome, not the regressors

Undergraduate courses teach attenuation from mismeasured x. The binding problem in welfare work is mismeasured y.

Consumption is not observed; it is reported. And how you ask changes the answer. In a randomised experiment run inside Susenas itself, the same households were assigned either a 23-question consumption module or a 320-question one:

23 questions instead of 320  →  consumption measured 12–20% lower

And the gap is not constant: a 1% rise in consumption raises the underestimate by about 0.4 percentage points. Richer households under-report proportionally more. Maximum understatement reaches ~23% for non-food, ~35% for food.

Why this matters for everything above. If y is measured with error that is correlated with y itself, then the “true” deciles against which we scored every model are themselves partly an artefact of the instrument. The reported error rates are a compound of model error and measurement error, and this pipeline cannot separate them.

Classical measurement error in y alone would inflate the residual variance and leave β unbiased. This is not classical: it is systematic and distribution-dependent, which is the harder case.

Source & method
Pradhan, M. (2001), Welfare Analysis with a Proxy Consumption Measure: Evidence from a Repeated Experiment in Indonesia, Cornell CFNPP Working Paper 126 — a randomised assignment of long and short consumption questionnaires within Susenas. Corroborating experimental evidence in Beegle, De Weerdt, Friedman & Gibson (2012), Journal of Development Economics 98(1), which finds recall modules measure lower consumption than a personal diary benchmark across eight methods in Tanzania.

In closing

Seven things this case teaches

Each is a general point in applied microeconometrics; each has a number from this one model attached to it.

1

Know which question you are asking

Prediction and causal inference share a command and almost nothing else. Coefficients here are weights, not effects.

2

More variables is not more information

Eight beat thirty-nine in sample. Two continuous consumption quantities outperform thirty-nine categorical indicators.

3

Functional form is a decision, not a default

Logs, squares and a +0.1 offset each change predictions. Declare them; a replicator who guesses differently gets different answers.

4

Know which R² you are quoting

LSDV 0.661 versus within 0.609 — identical estimates, and the choice reverses the apparent contribution of fixed effects.

5

Score on the decision, not the fit

Root MSE 0.365 against decile widths of 0.10–0.16: a respectable R² coexists with seven-in-ten wrong deciles.

6

Aggregates hide district failures

Papua looks acceptable in total while Yalimo is excluded wholesale. Always disaggregate the error, not just the estimate.

7

The outcome is measured, too

A shorter consumption module moves measured welfare 12–20%. Some of the “model error” above is really instrument error.

→

And one design implication

The two strongest predictors — metered electricity and telecom spending — are administratively verifiable. That makes an eight-question model a route to hybrid means testing, not merely a shorter survey.
