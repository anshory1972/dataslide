Study guide

MONASH-Health: reading one paper closely

Verikios, Dixon, Rimmer and Harris (2013): methodology and data

This guide works through a single paper in 20 study steps. It covers how the model is built and where its numbers come from. It does not summarise the paper; it takes the paper apart in the order in which its pieces depend on each other, and asks a question at each step so that understanding can be checked before moving on.

The paper matters here for two reasons. It is the method the Wellcome application cites for embedding health in an economywide model. It is also the closest published relative of the module being built in Work Package 2.3: the same labour-supply theory, a different country, and a much better data situation for the one quantity that is hardest to measure.

How to use this guide
  • Open the paper beside it. Every step names the section and page to read first. The guide explains; it does not replace.
  • Answer the check before opening it. Each step ends with one question. The answer is hidden until clicked.
  • If time is short: steps 3 to 6 are the labour-supply specification and steps 11 and 12 are the data behind it. Those six steps carry the method.

0The paper at a glance

Read this before starting. It is the map that the rest of the guide fills in.

One sentence to carry through the whole guide. Health enters this model at exactly one point, the transition matrix, and leaves it at exactly one point, the decision to offer labour. Everything between those two points is an ordinary computable general equilibrium model.

1Orientation

What the paper does and the six pieces of its labour-supply specification.

Covers steps 1 to 2

STUDY STEP 1

What the paper asks, and why it is the right paper to study

ReadAbstract and section 1, pages 1 to 3.

The paper builds a dynamic computable general equilibrium model of Australia in which the health status of the workforce determines labour supply. It then asks what happens to the economy if the rate of health decline falls permanently for one age group.

The answer is the paper's main contribution, and it is a comparison rather than a single number. A given health improvement given to older workers produces an effect about thirteen times larger than the same improvement given to younger workers. Employment rises by 0.13 per cent and real gross domestic product by 0.1 per cent for older workers; the corresponding figures for younger workers are 0.01 and 0.008 per cent.

This is the paper the Wellcome application cites for its method. It is also the closest published relative of the module being built in Work Package 2.3: same labour-supply theory, same treatment of health as a stock of people moving between states, a different country and a different data situation.

Why is the comparison between age groups the result, rather than the size of either effect?

Both effects are small in absolute terms, and both depend on an illustrative shock whose size the authors chose rather than estimated. What does not depend on the size of the shock is the ratio between the two: it comes from the difference in how steeply participation falls with poor health at different ages, which is measured from survey data. The ratio is the finding; the levels are an illustration.

STUDY STEP 2

The six ingredients of the labour-supply specification

ReadSection 3, first page, page 5, and the paper's Figure 1 on the same page.

Before any equation, the authors list the six things their specification must contain. Holding these six in mind makes the rest of section 3 straightforward, because each subsection supplies one or two of them.

  1. Categories, defined by age, health status and work status, fixed at the start of each year.
  2. Activities: what people actually do during the year.
  3. Labour supply from each category to each activity.
  4. Labour demand in the employment activities.
  5. Wage adjustment reflecting demand and supply.
  6. Who gets the jobs, and what happens to those who do not.

The paper's Figure 1, reproduced below, puts the first three on a timeline. The model has five age groups, five health statuses, eight occupations and three non-employment states, which are short-run unemployed, long-run unemployed and new entrants.

What is the difference between a category and an activity?

A category is a classification at a point in time, the start of the year, and it records what the person did in the previous year along with their age and health. An activity is what the person does during the year. The distinction matters because the module lets people offer labour from their category to any compatible activity, and the rationing of jobs then decides which offers are accepted.

2The labour-supply specification

Sections 3.1 to 3.3. Four equations. This is the part that is reused in Work Package 2.3.

Covers steps 3 to 6

STUDY STEP 3

Equation 1: how categories are produced from last year's activities

ReadSection 3.1, pages 5 and 6.

Equation 1CATt(a,h,c)=∑b∈AGE∑i∈HEALTHACTt−1(b,i,c)⋅T(b,i,c,a,h)

Categories from activities. The number of people in age group a, health status h and work status c at the start of year t is last year's activities summed over the age groups b and health statuses i they came from, weighted by the transition matrix.

Work status c is carried over unchanged: a person who was a manager last year enters this year in the manager category. Only age and health change in the transition. New entrants, those who were not in the labour force last year, are set exogenously from demographic data.

MechanismThis single equation is where a health policy enters the model. Nothing else in the specification needs to change: a policy is a change in the transition matrix T, and everything downstream follows.

If the transition matrix is left alone, what makes the category distribution change from year to year?

Ageing and the composition of last year's activities. People move up the age groups, some die or leave permanently, and the mix of activities in the previous year differs from the year before that. The matrix itself is a set of rates, not a forecast, so an unchanged matrix still produces a changing population.

STUDY STEP 4

Equation 2: the transition matrix is split into an age part and a health part

ReadSection 3.1, page 6.

Equation 2T(b,i,c,a,h)=P1(a|b,i)⋅P2(h|b,i,c)

The decomposition. The probability of moving from (b, i, c) to (a, h) is the probability of the age move times the probability of the health move.

The two parts are obtained in completely different ways, and this is the most instructive design decision in the paper.

  • The age part, P1. Almost mechanical. With ten-year age groups, a first approximation puts 0.9 of each group in the same group next year and 0.1 in the next group up. The second approximation multiplies both by one minus D, the probability of death or permanent discouragement from seeking work.
  • The health part, P2. Estimated from a household panel survey. This is the empirical core of the paper and the subject of study steps 11 and 12.

The authors note in a footnote that this makes T a first-order Markov model: the probability of being in a state this year depends only on the state last year, not on any earlier history.

Why does splitting T into two parts help?

The two components have different status. The age part is close to arithmetic and needs no data beyond a mortality and discouragement rate. The health part cannot be derived and must be measured. Separating them keeps the assumption confined to the part that is genuinely assumed, and lets the measured part carry the result.

STUDY STEP 5

Equation 3: how a category divides its labour among activities

ReadSection 3.2, pages 7 and 8.

Equation 3LSt(a,h,w,c)=CATt(a,h,w)⋅Fa,h,w,c(Wt(c)AWt(a,h,w))

Offers. Supply from a category to a labour force function is the size of the category, times a function F, times the wage in that activity relative to the average wage available to that category.

The function F carries four features of behaviour, and the authors state them explicitly:

  1. People who worked in an occupation last year supply strongly to the same occupation this year.
  2. People supply only to occupations compatible with their skills.
  3. Unemployed people supply relatively weakly, which is the discouraged worker effect.
  4. Supply depends on the age and health of the category.

Feature 4 is the one that carries the result of the whole paper, and it is not assumed. It is taken from the survey, and is the paper's Figure 2, reproduced below.

MechanismRead the two gradients off the chart. Within each age group the employment fraction falls as health worsens, and the fall is much sharper at H4 and H5 than between H1 and H3. Across age groups the whole profile drops after 48. The interaction of these two gradients is why the same health gain is worth more when given to older workers.

The paper's headline result is a ratio of about thirteen to one. Which piece of evidence produces it?

The employment fractions above. They are the only place where the sensitivity of participation to health is allowed to differ by age. The shock in the two simulations is deliberately identical in form, so the difference in the answers can only come from the difference in the employment-fraction gradients.

STUDY STEP 6

Equation 4: sluggish wages, and who gets the jobs

ReadSection 3.3, pages 9 and 10, including the paper's Figure 3.

Equation 4RWt(o)=RWt−1(o)+α[LDt(o)−LSt(o)]

Wage adjustment. The real after-tax wage for occupation o this year is last year's wage plus α times the gap between labour demand and labour supply in that occupation.

The paper sets α = 0.5. With sluggish wages the labour market does not clear, and supply normally exceeds demand. A rationing rule is therefore needed, and the authors state it in one sentence: incumbents have a strong advantage, and the job openings beyond those filled by incumbents are shared among the rest in proportion to the strength of their supply.

The paper's Figure 3 draws the resulting loop. Labour supply and labour demand both feed the real wage; the real wage feeds the core model; the core model produces industry outputs, prices, household income and gross domestic product; and labour demand comes back out of the core model.

Why does the model need a rationing rule at all?

Because the wage equation does not clear the market within the year. If wages adjusted fully, employment would equal supply and no rule would be needed. Under sluggish adjustment there are more people offering to work than there are positions, so the model must say explicitly who is employed. The choice made here, incumbents first, is what makes the transition gradual rather than immediate.

3The economywide model

Section 4. The health sector, the production nest, consumer demand, dynamics and solution.

Covers steps 7 to 10

STUDY STEP 7

The health sector: eighteen treatments and six services

ReadSection 4.1.1, page 11, and the paper's Table 2, reproduced below.

The detailed health sector is what makes this model different from earlier CGE work on health, which either buried health inside a broad sector or used a single-sector model.

Two distinct sets are involved, and confusing them makes the rest of section 4 hard to follow.

  • Eighteen health treatments, classified by the International Classification of Diseases, tenth revision. Cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, mental disorders, neoplasms and so on.
  • Six health commodities and services: human pharmaceuticals, hospitals and nursing homes, medical services, dental services, optometry, ambulance services.

MechanismThe treatment industries do not employ labour, capital or land directly. They buy health services as intermediate inputs, and it is the service industries that employ primary factors. So a treatment industry's demand for labour is indirect. Each treatment uses its own combination of services: respiratory disease and diabetes are pharmaceutical-intensive, injuries and neonatal care are hospital-intensive. The mix comes from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare.

Households buy the treatments at prices that carry a 65 per cent subsidy financed by government revenue. The same subsidy applies to the small amount of health services sold directly to households.

An improvement in health raises the number of healthy people. Does the model let treatment demand fall as a result?

No, and the authors identify this as a limitation in the conclusion. Household demand for treatments is exogenous and follows an external forecast. Because avoided illness does not reduce treatment spending, the model understates the economic gain from better health.

STUDY STEP 8

The production structure: three levels, and where the elasticities are

ReadSection 4.2.1, pages 13 and 14.

Forty-eight industries in total: eighteen treatments, six services and twenty-four non-health sectors. Each firm minimises cost under constant returns to scale, in three nested levels.

Equation 8qkiI=qi+akiI

Level 1, Leontief. Demand for each intermediate input moves with the industry's activity level, adjusted for input-specific technical change. Demand for the primary factor composite is specified the same way in equation 9. Fixing these as shares of output is what makes level 1 Leontief.

Equation 11qbiF=qiF−τ(pbiF−piF)

Level 2, substitution between primary factors. Land, labour and capital substitute with elasticity τ = 0.5. Equation 10, not shown, does the same for intermediate inputs between domestic and imported sources, with an elasticity of zero for every input except human pharmaceuticals, where it is 2.

Equation 12qniL=qbiF−υ(pniL−pbiF)

Level 3, substitution between occupations. The eight labour types substitute with elasticity υ = 0.35. This is the level at which the labour-supply module connects to the rest of the model.

Unless stated otherwise, the parameter values come from the MONASH model of Australia.

Why is the elasticity between domestic and imported sources zero for almost every intermediate input?

The paper does not argue the point; it takes the value from the parent model. The practical effect is that intermediate import shares are fixed, so the trade response in the results runs through final demand and through exports rather than through input sourcing. The single exception, human pharmaceuticals at 2, is the one intermediate input where import substitution is allowed to matter.

STUDY STEP 9

Consumer demand, and the awkward case of health treatments

ReadSection 4.2.2, pages 15 and 16.

Equation 13qiH−w=εi(vH−b)+∑g=148ηigpgH+aiH−aH

Household demand. A Klein-Rubin system: demand for each of the 48 goods responds to total household expenditure, to prices, and to tastes. Expenditure elasticities run from 0 to 1.35 and own-price elasticities from −0.84 to −0.27.

For non-health goods and for health services, tastes are exogenous and demand responds to expenditure and prices in the usual way. Health treatments are handled in the opposite direction, and the reason is instructive.

MechanismSome treatments would have zero demand at a zero price if nobody were ill, so an ordinary demand function is not a good description. The authors therefore make household tastes endogenous and set the quantity of treatments exogenously, following forecasts of future demand for individual treatments built from Australian Institute of Health and Welfare data. Suppliers then meet that demand through market clearing.

LimitationThe authors are explicit that this is a compromise. In reality treatment demand is rationed, usually by queueing, which imposes time costs and the disutility of delay that the model does not represent. They state that further work is required, and argue the representation does not undermine the paper's use of the model for health status changes.

What does it mean that the quantity of treatments is exogenous while its taste variable is endogenous?

It means the demand equation is being solved backwards. Instead of using tastes and prices to determine quantity, the model takes the quantity from a forecast and lets the taste term absorb whatever is required to make the equation hold. The equation is still satisfied, but it no longer carries behavioural content for those goods.

STUDY STEP 10

Dynamics and how the model is solved

ReadSections 4.3 and 4.4, pages 16 and 17.

Three dynamic mechanisms operate. Capital accumulates industry by industry, with investment responding to the expected rate of return. Liabilities accumulate for the public sector and the foreign account. Lagged adjustment covers the wage equation already seen, and the gradual elimination of differences in rates of return across industries.

The model is written as a system of equations in the levels, then totally differentiated so that it can be solved in percentage-change form. This is the method of Johansen; accuracy is maintained by multistep solution procedures in GEMPACK.

MechanismEvery simulation is run twice. The baseline is a plausible business-as-usual forecast in which exogenous variables follow expert projections. The policy run repeats it with the variables of interest changed. The result is the difference between the two. For this paper the only difference between the runs is the set of exogenous variables representing transitions across health statuses.

If the baseline were changed, would the reported results change?

Yes, and this is a general property of this class of model rather than a fault of the paper. The reported numbers are deviations from a particular baseline. A baseline with a different age structure or a different starting distribution of health would give a different-sized response to the same shock, because the number of people in each category at the moment of the shock would differ.

4The data

Where every number comes from, and what the survey actually shows.

Covers steps 11 to 13

STUDY STEP 11

The transition data: five waves of HILDA

ReadSection 3.1, page 7, and the paper's Table 1, reproduced in full below.

The health part of the transition matrix comes from the Household, Income and Labour Dynamics in Australia survey, a nationally representative household panel run annually since 2001. The authors used the first five available waves, covering 2002 to 2006.

The procedure has three steps, and it is short enough to state completely. Take the self-reported health status of individuals who appear in both year t−1 and year t. Record the transition. Average over all available transitions.

What makes this possible is the panel. The same person is observed in two consecutive years, so the transition is measured directly. This is the single largest methodological difference between this paper and the Indonesian module in Work Package 2.3, where no such survey exists and the transition rates must be recovered from a sequence of cross-sections instead.

Table 1 of the paper is the result: a 5 by 5 matrix of health transitions for each of the five age groups, separately for working and non-working people. Study step 12 examines it.

The classification in Table 1 has only two work statuses, working and non-working, but the model has eleven. Why?

Sample size. A transition matrix estimated separately for each of the eight occupations and three non-employment states would rest on very few observations per cell. The authors therefore let health transitions vary between working and non-working people only. They state the consequence in section 5.5: because transitions do not vary by occupation, the health improvement does not favour the supply of labour to any particular occupation.

STUDY STEP 12

Reading Table 1: what the survey actually shows

ReadTable 1 of the paper, page 8. Use the interactive version below to move through it.

Two patterns run through the whole table, and both matter for the result.

  1. Working people decline less. For every age group except the youngest, people who worked in year t−1 are less likely to report worse health in year t than people who did not. Among 39 to 48 year olds in excellent health, 49 per cent of workers decline against 67 per cent of non-workers.
  2. The exception is the young. Non-working people aged 15 to 28 in excellent health are less likely to decline than their working counterparts, 44 per cent against 48. The authors suggest this group is dominated by students.

LimitationThe direction of causation is not established by these numbers. Working people may stay healthier because they work, or they may work because they are healthier. The model takes the transitions as given and does not resolve this.

In the model, does the fact that working people decline less create a feedback loop?

Yes, and it is worth being clear about it. Better health raises participation through the employment fractions. Participation then lowers the rate of health decline, because the transition matrix for working people is more favourable. That feeds back into the health distribution the following year. The effect is built into the structure through the work-status index on P2.

Health treatment industries show A$49 billion of inputs and A$47 billion of sales. What does that tell you about their structure?

That they are almost pure intermediaries. They buy health services, add very little else, and sell the result to households. They use no primary factors directly. The labour embodied in a treatment is the labour used by the service industries that supply it.

STUDY STEP 14

The experiment: what exactly was shocked

ReadSection 5, pages 17 and 18.

Two simulations, identical in form, differing only in which age group receives the improvement.

Simulation 1Simulation 2
Age group improvedA49-60, older workersA29-38, younger workers
Who is affected10 per cent of people in that age group with health status H4 or H5
What changesThose people move one health status better than they were destined to
How it entersBy shocking T(b,i,c,a,h) in equation 1
TimingPermanent, starting in 2011

MechanismThe phrase to hold onto is one status better than they were destined to. The shock does not move people between health states directly. It alters where they were going to end up. Someone in H4 who would have become H5 becomes H4; someone who would have stayed H4 becomes H3, and so on up the scale. This is why the authors describe it as a reduction in the rate of health decline rather than as an improvement in health.

The authors are careful to say the 10 per cent figure is illustrative and corresponds to no particular programme. They then show it is not unreasonable: it is equivalent to improving the health of about 17 per cent of people with chronic hypertension, or 10 per cent of those with cardiovascular disease.

Why shock the transition matrix rather than the number of people in each health state?

Because a one-off move between states would wash out. The population would return towards its old distribution as the unchanged transition rates reasserted themselves. Changing the rate of decline is permanent by construction, so the improvement persists and accumulates as successive cohorts pass through the improved transition.

STUDY STEP 15

The first-round effect: who moves, and how many

ReadSection 5.1, pages 18 and 19, and the paper's Table 4, reproduced below.

The shock moves about 19,300 older workers from the poor health statuses H4 and H5 into H1 to H3, and about 12,100 younger workers. The ratio of younger to older movers is only 0.62, and the reason is compositional: 19 per cent of older workers hold H4 or H5 against 11 per cent of younger workers, even though younger workers are the larger group, 20 per cent of the workforce against 16 per cent.

The second compositional fact runs the other way. Of the older workers who move, about 63 per cent are employed; of the younger movers, about 86 per cent. Older people in poor health are much less likely to be working in the first place, which is exactly why there is more room for their participation to rise.

More younger movers are already employed. Does that make the younger simulation stronger or weaker?

Weaker. A person who is already employed contributes little additional labour supply when their health improves. The gain comes from people who were not working and now do. The older group has both more movers and a larger share of non-workers among them, so it produces the larger supply response twice over.

STUDY STEP 16

The labour market in the first year

ReadSections 5.2 and 5.3, pages 19 to 21, with the paper's Tables 5 and 6, both reproduced below.

Table 5 of the paper separates the two sides of the first-round effect, and the separation is the clearest thing in the paper. Offers from workers who moved into the good health statuses rise by about 1 per cent in simulation 1 and 0.45 per cent in simulation 2. Offers from the depleted poor health statuses fall by about 4 per cent in both.

The net effect is small and is what remains after these two large opposing movements: labour supply from older workers rises by about 0.11 per cent, and from younger workers by about 0.01 per cent.

In 2011, per centSimulation 1, olderSimulation 2, younger
Labour supply, all workers+0.016+0.002
Real wage rate−0.006−0.001
Labour demand+0.005+0.001

MechanismThe causal chain in the first year is short. More supply at the initial wage creates an excess supply of labour. Wages fall, but under equation 4 they fall sluggishly and do not clear the market. The lower wage induces firms to expand labour demand. Employment therefore rises by much less than supply does in the first year.

Labour supply rises by 0.016 per cent but demand by only 0.005. Where did the rest go?

It did not become employment in 2011. It is absorbed as excess supply, because the wage adjusts by only half the gap each year. Over the following years the wage continues to fall and the gap closes, which is why the long-run response in Figure 4 is larger than the first-year response.

STUDY STEP 17

The macroeconomic path to 2030

ReadSection 5.4, pages 21 to 23, and the paper's Figure 4, reproduced below.

By 2030, per cent deviationSimulation 1, olderSimulation 2, younger
Labour supply+0.13+0.01
Capital+0.08+0.006
Real gross domestic product+0.10+0.008
Real wage−0.115small
Real exchange rate−0.17−0.014

MechanismThe chain from labour supply to the exchange rate is worth following once, because it explains the sectoral results in the next step. More labour lowers the real wage. Lower wages lower domestic production costs and therefore domestic prices. The consumer price index is the numeraire and cannot fall, so the nominal exchange rate depreciates instead. Domestic goods become cheaper relative to imported goods, which is the real depreciation. Import prices rise in domestic currency, so the terms of trade fall.

Capital rises by less than labour, so the capital-labour ratio falls and, through the factor-price frontier, the return to capital rises relative to labour. Real gross domestic product therefore expands by less than labour usage does.

Labour supply rises by 0.13 per cent but GDP by only 0.10 per cent. Why is GDP not proportional?

Because capital does not keep pace. Investment responds to rates of return and accumulates over time, so the capital stock rises by only 0.08 per cent. Output is produced by both factors, so it grows at a rate between the two. If capital adjusted fully, GDP would rise roughly in proportion to labour.

STUDY STEP 18

The sectoral results, and why traded sectors gain most

ReadSection 5.5, pages 23 to 25, and the paper's Figure 5. Its panel (a) is covered by Table A above.

By 2030 in simulation 1, secondary industries expand by 0.25 per cent, primary industries by 0.17 per cent and tertiary industries by only 0.08 per cent.

MechanismTraded sectors face more elastic demand in world markets, so when domestic costs fall they gain more than sectors selling at home. Among the traded sectors, manufacturing gains more than agriculture and mining because it uses more labour, so a fall in the wage reduces its costs by more.

The effects on occupational demands are small and similar across occupations. Intermediate production and transport workers gain most, because they are employed chiefly by the secondary industries; professionals and clerical workers gain least, because they are employed chiefly by services.

A discrepancy inside the paper, worth knowing about. The text on page 25 gives the highest occupational growth in simulation 1 as 0.07 per cent and the lowest as 0.04 per cent. The vertical axis of panel (b) of its Figure 5 runs to 0.18, and the curves end between about 0.11 and 0.17. The two statements do not agree. The ranking of occupations is the same in both, so treat the ranking as the finding and not the level.

LimitationThe authors explain why the occupational spread is narrow, and the explanation is an assumption, not a finding. Health transitions do not vary by occupation, and industries do not distinguish labour inputs by age or health status. Relative wages across occupations therefore move together, and demands cannot diverge much.

If industries could distinguish workers by age and health, what would change?

Wage rates would differ by age and health status, which would create a direct link between the composition of labour supply and industry output. An industry employing many older or less healthy workers would see its costs change by more than others. The authors name this as the first of two areas for future development.

7Judgement

What the model cannot do, and how it compares with the Indonesian module.

Covers steps 19 to 20

STUDY STEP 19

What the authors say the model cannot do

ReadSection 6, pages 25 and 26.

Two limitations are stated by the authors themselves, and both are worth carrying into any use of this framework.

  1. Industries do not distinguish labour by age or health status. Wages do not vary along those dimensions, so there is no direct mechanism from the composition of labour supply to industry output.
  2. Health treatments are exogenous to health status. Better health does not reduce treatment demand, so the model understates the economic gains from improved health.

A third limitation is implied rather than stated. Health status is self-reported on a five-point scale. Nothing in the model connects a health status to a disease, so the shock cannot be traced back to a specific intervention. The authors acknowledge this indirectly when they describe their 10 per cent improvement as corresponding to no particular programme.

Which of these limitations would most affect a study of air pollution and health?

The third. A pollution study needs a route from exposure to a specific disease and from that disease to a health state. A self-reported five-point scale with no disease detail cannot provide that route on its own; it has to be supplied from outside the model, which is exactly how the Indonesian module treats it.

STUDY STEP 20

The structural point. Everything in the middle column is the same machinery as the right-hand column. The differences are in the data available and in three additions. Reading this paper closely is therefore the cheapest way to understand what the Indonesian module will do, and where it departs from an approach that has already been published and reviewed.

The Indonesian module has worse transition data but better participation data. Why does that trade look favourable?

Because the participation gradient is what carries the result, as study step 5 showed, and it is measured directly in both cases. The transition rates set the size of the population flow; the participation gradient sets how much labour supply that flow produces. Having the weaker source for the first and a direct measurement for the second puts the uncertainty in the less decisive place.

AAppendix A. Glossary

Terms as this paper uses them. Where the paper's usage differs from the Indonesian module, the difference is noted.

Activity
What a person does during the year: one of 8 occupations, short-run unemployment, long-run unemployment, or being a new entrant.
Category
A classification at the start of the year, by age group, health status and the work status held in the previous year.
Employment fraction
The share of a group classified by age and health status that is employed. Shown in Figure 2 of the paper. The single most important empirical input.
H1 to H5
Self-reported health status: excellent, very good, good, fair, poor.
Health decline
The probability of moving to a worse health status within one year. The final column of Table 1 of the paper.
Health treatment
One of 18 industries classified by disease. Buys health services; uses no primary factors directly.
Health service
One of 6 industries, such as hospitals or pharmaceuticals. Uses labour and capital and sells almost entirely to the treatment industries.
HILDA
Household, Income and Labour Dynamics in Australia: a nationally representative household panel survey, annual since 2001. Follows the same person over time, which is what makes transitions observable.
Incumbent
A person who held a job in the previous year. Favoured in the rationing of positions.
Klein-Rubin
The utility function behind the household demand system in equation 13. It implies a subsistence quantity for each good and expenditure elasticities that are constant.
Numeraire
The price held fixed so that all other prices are measured against it. Here it is the consumer price index, which is why a fall in domestic costs appears as a depreciation of the nominal exchange rate.
P1, P2
The two parts of the transition matrix: the probability of the age move, and the probability of the health move.
Rationing
The rule deciding who is employed when labour supply exceeds labour demand. Needed because wages adjust sluggishly.
Terms of trade
The price of exports relative to the price of imports. Falls in both simulations because import prices rise in domestic currency.
Transition matrix
The probabilities of moving between age and health states within one year, written T in equation 1.
