Summer School 2026

The Growth We Want

Arief Anshory Yusuf Bio ↗

Inclusive, sustainable, and long overdue. The charts below diagnose why the old growth model stopped working for most people — rising inequality, a hollowed-out middle of the job market, and a populist backlash that tracks it closely — before turning to what a better growth path has to deliver instead: broadly shared gains and a model the planet can actually sustain. Every chart was rebuilt from its own primary source — pulled live from the World Bank, OECD, FRED and WID.world APIs, merged from ParlGov and PopuList election records, read out of an OECD StatLink spreadsheet, or, where a dataset stayed locked behind a credential, traced from the original publication and labelled as an estimate.

Geopolitical risk, same window as EPU

Geopolitical risk has climbed alongside economic policy uncertainty

Caldara & Iacoviello Geopolitical Risk Index (historical series), annual average, 2000–2025

Source & notes
Source: Caldara & Iacoviello, “Measuring Geopolitical Risk,” American Economic Review 112, no. 4 (2022) — historical index (GPRH, 1900–2019=100), built from the frequency of newspaper coverage of geopolitical tension, war, and terrorism. Pulled directly from the authors’ own public dataset (matteoiacoviello.com/gpr.htm). This is the sister index to the Trade Policy Uncertainty series in the same IMF report Figure 7 was traced from (IMF SDN/2023/001, Figure 6). Cropped to 2000–2025 to match the EPU chart alongside it. Visible spikes: 9/11 and Afghanistan (2001, 111), the Iraq War (2003, 135), and a sustained climb from 2022 (Ukraine, Middle East conflicts, tariff escalation) to 116 in 2025. For context, the fuller series (not shown here) reaches 200–380 during WWI/WWII — several times any level seen since. Today’s uncertainty is real and rising — but not, on this measure, unprecedented.

Slowbalization

World trade has stalled as a share of GDP since 2008

World Bank WDI, NE.TRD.GNFS.ZS, world aggregate, 1970–2024

Source & notes
Source: World Bank World Development Indicators, pulled live via the WDI API (country=WLD). Trade rose steadily from ~27% of world GDP in 1970 to a peak of ~61% in 2008, then plateaued and has oscillated in a band roughly 51–62% ever since — the "slowbalization" pattern: trade didn't reverse, but its multi-decade rise essentially stopped after the Global Financial Crisis.

Longest available range · same series

Top 1% income share, the full WID history: 1820–2024

Same nine countries and the same WID.world series as the chart above — extended back to the earliest year WID carries for it

Source & notes
Source: World Inequality Database, series sptinc992j (share of pretax national income, equal-split adults), percentile p99p100, same pull via WID.world’s official wid R package as the chart above. Coverage this early is sparse (a handful of benchmark years per century, reconstructed from historical tax and wealth records) and lower-confidence than the dense post-1980 data — treat the pre-1900 portion as indicative of the broad level and trend, not a precise annual series. Density picks up from the early 20th century onward as income-tax records begin, and becomes near-annual from the 1980s.

Why the mid-century compression, and why it lasted:
Shock (1929–45): Depression destroyed capital income (dividends/profits, most of the top 1%) faster than wages; WWII added >90% top tax rates, wage controls, and — especially in Germany, France, Japan — physical/financial destruction of capital.
Why it held for 30 years, not just wartime: peacetime govts kept top rates near 90% into the 1960s–70s; Bretton Woods capital controls (1944–71) kept finance small; unions stayed strong, keeping bargaining power with labor.
Why it reversed post-1980: tax cuts (Reagan/Thatcher era), capital-account liberalization, and de-unionization removed all three props at once.

Increase of right-wing populism

Far-right vote share has risen roughly ten-fold since the 1980s

Real election vote shares (ParlGov) classified by party family (PopuList), 30 European countries, 5-year averages

Source & notes
This is an original reconstruction, not a copy of the slide's exact category scheme. Built by merging two real datasets: ParlGov's actual national-parliament election vote shares (via parlgov.fly.dev, a working ParlGov mirror, after the main site's Cloudflare blocked direct access) with PopuList v4.0's party classification (far-right/far-left/populist flags with active-year ranges, matched on each party's parlgov_id). My classification rule, applied per party per election year: far-right if PopuList flags it far-right in that year; else far-left if flagged far-left; else "other populist" if flagged populist but not far-left/far-right; else mainstream. Vote shares are averaged in 5-year bins across all ~30 PopuList-covered European countries' national elections (636 elections, 1900–2023). Far-right vote share: ~1–2% through the 1970s–80s, rising past 10% by 2010 and reaching ~14% in the 2015–19 bin.

The great decoupling

Productivity has outpaced compensation since the late 1990s

OECD Compendium of Productivity Indicators 2018, Figure 5.5 StatLink data, unweighted average of 20 OECD countries, 1995–2016

Source & notes
Source: not a re-derivation this time — this is the actual OECD StatLink Excel file behind Figure 5.5 of the OECD Compendium of Productivity Indicators 2018 (http://dx.doi.org/10.1787/888933734303), which every chart/table in that publication carries at the bottom of the page. Chart 6 · Who actually gained from globalization

The Elephant Curve, rebuilt from the original decile data

Annualized real income growth by global income percentile, 1988–2008 — Lakner & Milanovic's World Panel Income Distribution

Source & notes
Built from scratch, not traced from Milanovic's published image: real country-decile income and population data from the World Bank's Lakner-Milanovic World Panel Income Distribution dataset (mysample==1, benchmark years 1988 and 2008; 750 country-decile observations in 1988, 1,209 in 2008, covering 4.1–6.1 billion people). Every country-decile pair was pooled, population-weighted, and ranked globally by income to build the 1988 and 2008 global income distributions independently, then linearly interpolated to whole percentiles 1–99 to compute each percentile's annualized real growth rate. Because deciles (not finer percentiles) are the finest grain available per country, this is smoother/coarser than Milanovic's original 100-bin version but reproduces the same shape: strong growth (2–3%/yr) through the emerging-economy middle of the distribution (largely China, India, and other Asian economies), a real dip to roughly flat or slightly negative around the 80th–85th global percentile (the "elephant's trunk" — rich-country lower-middle classes), then a renewed rise into the global top percentiles.

Sources & methodology (applies to all charts above)
Source: World Bank World Development Indicators API, pulled directly — NY.GDP.PCAP.KD (GDP per capita, constant 2015 US$) and the Poverty and Inequality Platform headcount ratios SI.POV.DDAY ($2.15/day, 2017 PPP), SI.POV.LMIC ($3.65/day), SI.POV.UMIC ($6.85/day), all countries, 1960–2024. Spells are consecutive available survey-year pairs per country (not all possible pairs), so each spell is independent within its own country's sequence. Aggregates and regional groupings excluded — country-level observations only. Growth is annualized as (GDP₃/GDP₁)^(1/gap) − 1; the poverty change is the simple annualized percentage-point difference, following Ravallion's convention of working in levels rather than logs (poverty rates near zero make log growth rates unstable).
