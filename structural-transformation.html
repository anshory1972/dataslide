Agriculture’s share of value added and income per head

Source & method
Countries: World Bank World Development Indicators, GDP per capita (constant 2015 US$, NY.GDP.PCAP.KD) and agriculture, forestry and fishing value added as a share of GDP (NV.AGR.TOTL.ZS); most recent year reporting both, 2015–2024, 202 countries. Indonesia’s path: the same two series for 1983–2024. The dashed segment, 1960–1982, uses agriculture value added over GDP with both in constant local currency (NV.AGR.TOTL.KD / NY.GDP.MKTP.KD), which differs from the current-price measure by 0.8 percentage points on average across the 42 overlapping years. Provinces: BPS, PDRB by 17 industry categories at current prices and GRDP per capita, 2024. Provincial income per head is placed on the same axis by scaling each province’s current-rupiah GRDP per head by the ratio of Indonesia’s 2024 GDP per head in constant 2015 US dollars to its 2024 GDP per head in current rupiah, so provincial relativities are those BPS reports and the national level is the World Bank’s.

Manufacturing’s share of value added and income per head

Source & method
Provinces: BPS, PDRB by 17 industry categories at current prices and GRDP per capita, every year from 2010 — the first year of the 2010-base regional accounts, with no earlier provincial breakdown. Each year’s income per head is converted with that year’s own anchor, the ratio of Indonesia’s GDP per head in constant 2015 US dollars to its GDP per head in current rupiah. The four provinces created in 2022–2023 appear as single points in 2024. Indonesia and the country groups: World Bank, manufacturing value added as a share of GDP (NV.IND.MANF.ZS) against GDP per capita in constant 2015 US dollars. Lower-middle income runs 1965–2024 and peaks at 24.1% in 1979; East Asia and Pacific developing runs 2004–2024 and peaks at 30.6% in 2006; the world as a whole falls from 19.2% in 1997 to 14.9%. The three peer economies peak and fall too: China 32.0% in 2006 to 24.8%, Malaysia 30.9% in 1999 to 22.5%, Thailand 30.9% in 2010 to 24.2%. Marked points on each line are its first year, its peak and 2024. Summing provincial PDRB gives a manufacturing share 2.6 points above the national figure in 2010, widening to 4.3 points by 2024, because provincial accounts and national GDP differ in coverage and in the treatment of taxes on products.

Output per head and poverty

Source & method
GRDP per capita: BPS variable 288, at constant 2010 prices, rebased to constant 2012 prices by multiplying by Indonesia’s implicit GDP deflator for 2012 (1.1211, computed from the national current-price and constant-price series), so that it sits on the same price base as the expenditure series. The national deflator is applied to the provinces as well, since the expenditure measure is itself adjusted to a single national basis. Adjusted expenditure per capita: BPS variable 416, the consumption component of the new-method Human Development Index, already published at constant 2012 prices and adjusted for purchasing-power differences between regions. Poverty: BPS variable 192, percentage below the poverty line (P0), urban and rural combined, March; the provincial March series begins in 2012. Gini: BPS variable 98, urban and rural combined, March, multiplied by 100 to read as a percentage. March is used throughout because BPS also publishes a September round and the two are not comparable.

The challenge of global fragmentation

Source & method
Trade: World Bank, trade as a share of world GDP, 1970–2024. Trade restrictions: Global Trade Alert, count of new discriminatory interventions imposed worldwide each year, split into goods, services and investment, 2009–2022. Far-right vote share: ParlGov national-parliament election results, share of the vote won by parties classified as far right, five-yearly from 1900 to 2020. Seat share: most recent lower-house election per country as of writing — Austria 2024, Italy 2022, Portugal 2025, Netherlands 2023, Germany 2025, Finland 2023, France 2024, Sweden 2022, Belgium 2024, Spain 2023, Poland 2023; national election results via IPU Parline. A country’s largest far-right party is not always in government even where it holds a large seat share. All four panels are taken from The Growth We Want.
